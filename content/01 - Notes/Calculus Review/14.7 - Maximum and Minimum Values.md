# Maximums and Minimums

## Local Max and Local Min

> [!definition]
> A function of two variables $f$ has a local maximum at $(a,b)$ if $f(x,y) \leq f(a,b)$ when $(x,y)$ is near $(a,b)$. This means that $f(x,y) \leq f(a,b)$ for all points $(x,y)$ in some disk with center $(a,b)$. The function $f$ has a local minimum at $(a,b)$ if $f(x,y) \geq f(a,b)$ when $(x,y)$ is near $(a,b)$.

### Finding Extrema

The following theorem is analogous to Fermat's Theorem but for multivariable functions:

> [!theorem]
> If $f$ has a local maximum or minimum at $(a,b)$ and the first-order derivatives exist there, then $f_{x}(a,b) = 0$ and $f_{y}(a,b) = 0$

**Proof:** 

Assume $f$ has a max at $(a,b)$: Then let $g(x) = f(x,b)$ which is the equivalent of holding $y$ fixed at $y=b$. If $f$ has a maximum at $(a,b)$ then $g$ has a maximum at $a$, which by Fermat's Theorem states $g'(a) = 0$ but $g'(a) = f_{x}(a, b)$. Similarly, let $h(y) = f(a,y)$ and since $f$ has a max at $(a,b)$ then $h$ has a maximum at $b$ which by the same theorem says $h'(b) = 0$. But $h'(b) = f_{y}(a,b)$. Hence, it must be that $f_{x}(a, b) = 0$ and $f_{y}(a,b) = 0$ when $f$ has a maximum at $(a,b)$. 

---

A point $(a,b)$ is called a **critical point** if $\nabla f(a,b) = \vec{0}$ or one of the partial derivatives of $f$ does not exist, since a sign change may occur for $\nabla f(x,y)$ when $(x,y) \ne (a,b)$. So if $f$ has a local maximum or minimum, it happens wherever there's a critical point. But the converse is not true: Not all critical points lead to a minimum or maximum. 

**Problem:** Find the extreme values of $f(x,y) = y^{2} - x^{2}$.

**Approach:** Let's find the critical point for $f$ by finding $f_{x}$ and $f_{y}$ and setting them to zero.

$$
\begin{align}
 & f_{x} = -2x & f_{x} = 0 \to x = 0 \\ \\
 & f_{y} = 2y & f_{y} = 0 \to y = 0
\end{align}
$$

So a critical point for $f$ is the origin on the $xy$-plane $(0,0)$. 

Suppose we run $f$ along the $x$-axis, that is letting $x = 0$, so $f$ becomes $f(y) = y^{2}$. For values of $y \ne 0$, $f(y) > f(0)$. If we run $f$ along the $y$-axis, letting $y = 0$, $f$ becomes $f(x) = -x^{2}$ which for values of $x$ where $x \ne 0$ gives $f(x) < f(0)$. So from different directions of $f$, we have values greater than and less than $f(0,0)$, making $f(0,0)$ neither a local maximum nor minimum.

When a critical point yields neither a local maximum nor minimum, we call that point a **saddle point**. The geometric intuition is that it is the highest point of the graph of $f$ running along one axis, however is the lowest point of the graph of $f$ running along another axis.  

### Second Derivative Test

> [!theorem]
> Suppose the second partial derivatives of $f$ are continuous on a disk with center $(a,b)$ and suppose $\nabla f(a,b) = \vec{0}$ meaning $(a,b)$ is a critical point. Let
> 
> $$ D = D(a,b) = f_{xx}(a,b)f_{yy}(a,b) - []f_{xy}(a,b)]^{2} $$
> 
> * If $D>0$ and $f_{xx}(a,b)<0$ then $f(a,b)$ is a local maximum.
> * If $D > 0$ and $f_{xx}(a,b) > 0$ then $f(a,b)$ is a local minimum.
> * If $D < 0$, then $(a,b)$ is a saddle point of $f$.

If $D = 0$, then the test gives no information as to whether $(a,b)$ is where an extrema lives or is a saddle point for $f$. We'd need to use other information to determine extrema.
#### Geometric Intuition

If $f$ really had an extrema at some point $(a,b)$ we'd want the concavity in both the $x$ and $y$ directions to agree. When $f$ has a local minimum at $(a,b)$ then it's concave up in both directions, that is $f_{x x}(a,b) > 0$ and $f_{yy}(a,b) > 0$ which implies $f_{x}(a,b)f_{y}(a,b) > 0$; similar when $f$ has a local maximum at $(a,b)$ then $f_{x x}(a,b) < 0$ and $f_{yy}(a,b) < 0$ implying $f_{ x x}f_{yy} > 0$. Otherwise, when $f$ has a saddle point at $(a,b)$, we'd be running along one direction where it's concave up and running along another where it's concave down which means $f_{x x}f_{yy} < 0$. So our goal is to check the signs of the product between $f_{x x}$ and $f_{yy}$ at the point of interest $(a,b)$. That's the first part of the Second Derivative Test.

Taken from the Stewart's Textbook:

![[Pasted image 20260720144524.png]]

Imagine the graph/surface of $f$ represents the height of the canyon in the above photo where a saddle point exists. Suppose we travel the mountain, starting at the saddle point, along the $x$-direction and then move a little to the $y$-direction. We find that the steepness of the mountain is greater than just having ran only along the $x$-direction; that is $|f_{xy}| > |f_{x}|$ or if we moved along the $y$-direction $|f_{yx}| > |f_{y}|$. Graphically, that represents the graph of $f$ twisting or bending in the $xy$-direction. Since $f_{xy} = f_{yx}$ and all sides of the inequalities are positive, we can multiply both inequalities to get

$$
|f_{xy}| \cdot |f_{xy}| > |f_{x x}| \cdot |f_{yy}| \to (f_{xy})^{2} - |f_{xx}||f_{yy}| > 0 \quad \text{or} \quad |f_{x x}||f_{y y}| - (f_{xy})^{2} < 0
$$

Incorporating both parts, we want to check at the point $(a,b)$ if

$$
f_{x x} f_{yy} - (f_{xy})^{2} > 0
$$

If this inequality is true, that means there's an extreme value at $(a,b)$. One of the second derivatives $f_{x x}$ serves as our proxy for the general shape of $f$: If $f_{x x} > 0$, that means the graph of $f$ is concave up corresponding to a minimum, and if $f_{x x} < 0$, then the graph of $f$ is concave down corresponding to a maximum. There's no difference if we used $f_{yy}$ since this inequality being true means $f_{xx}$ has the same sign as $f_{yy}$. 

> [!note]
> It's helpful to remember $D$ as a determinant
> 
> $$
> D = \begin{vmatrix}
> f_{xx} & f_{yx} \\
> f_{xy} & f_{yy}
> \end{vmatrix}
> = f_{xx}f_{yy} - (f_{xy})^{2}
> $$
> 
> We are in fact taking the determinant of a matrix called the **Hessian matrix** $H$, whose entries contain the second- and mixed-partials of $f$. So $D = \det(H)$.

---

**Problem:** Find any minimum or maximum values as well as any saddle points of the function

$$
f(x,y) = xy - 2x - 2y - x^{2} - y^{2}
$$

**Approach:** Taking the first-order partials, 

$$
\begin{align}
f_{x} = y - 2 - 2x \\ \\
f_{y} = x - 2 - 2y
\end{align}
$$

Our goal is to find any points which zero out $f_{x}$ and $f_{y}$ simultaneously. That's done by solving the system

$$
\begin{align}
f_{x} = 0  \to y - 2 - 2x = 0\\ \\
f_{y} = 0 \to x - 2 - 2y = 0
\end{align} \quad
$$

If $f_{x}$ is set to zero, then $y = 2(1+x)$ which can be incorporated into the second equation $f_{y} = 0$.

$$
\begin{align}
 & x - 2 - 4(1+x) = 0 \\ \\
 & x - 2 - 4 - 4x = 0 \\ \\
 & -3x = 6 \\ \\
 & x = -2 \to y = 2(1 - 2) = -2
\end{align}
$$

So a critical point of $f$ is $(-2, -2)$. 

Our next step is to take the Second Derivative Test which requires to take $f_{xx}$, $f_{xy}$, and $f_{yy}$.

$$
f_{xx} = -2 \quad f_{xy} = 1 \quad f_{yy} = -2
$$

> The second derivatives and mixed partials are constant throughout $x$ and $y$. 

So,

$$
D(-2,-2) = -2(-2) - 1^{2} = 4 - 1 = 3 > 0
$$

and since $f_{xx} = -2 < 0$, then a local maximum exists at $(-2,-2)$. 

Calculating $f(-2,-2)$, 

$$
\begin{align}
f(-2,-2) &  = -2(-2) - 2(-2) - 2(-2) - (2)^{2} - (2)^{2} \\ \\
 & = 4 + 4 + 4 - 4 - 4 \\ \\
 & = 4
\end{align}
$$

In summary, $f(-2, -2)$ is a local maximum. 

## Absolute Max and Absolute Min

> [!definition]
> Let $(a,b)$ be a point in the domain $D$ for function $f$ of two variables. Then $f(a,b)$ is the 
> 
> * absolute maximum value of $f$ on $D$ if $f(a,b) \geq f(x,y)$ for all $(x,y)$ in $D$.
> * absolute minimum value of $f$ on $D$ if $f(a,b) \leq f(x,y)$ for all $(x,y)$ in $D$.

### Extension of Extreme Value Theorem

A **closed set** in $R^{2}$ is one which contains all its boundary points.

![[Pasted image 20260720153529.png|400x400]]

A **bounded set** in $R^{2}$ is one which is contained in some disk; moreover, the bounded set is finite. An example of a closed, bounded set includes

$$
D = \{ {(x,y)} \ | \ {x^{2} + y^{2} \leq 1} \}
$$

The following is analogous to the original Extreme Value Theorem. 

> [!theorem]
> If $f$ is continuous on a closed, bounded set $D$ in $R^{2}$, then $f$ attains an absolute maximum value $f(x_{1},y_{1})$ and an absolute minimum value $f(x_{2},y_{2})$ at some points $(x_{1},y_{1})$ and $(x_{2},y_{2})$ in $D$.

So the process for finding extreme values in some closed set $D$ involves

1. Finding local extrema whose points live in $D$
2. Finding max/min values along the boundaries of $D$
3. Evaluating the absolute max/min values amongst the extrema and boundaries. 

**Problem:** Find the absolute maximum and minimum values of $f$ on the set $D$ where $f(x,y) = x^{2} + y^{2} - 2x$ and $D$ is the closed triangular region with vertices $(2,0)$, $(0,2)$, and $(0, -2)$. 

**Approach:** Let's find the local extrema of $f$ which live in the domain $D$. 

Taking the first-order partials of $f$, 

$$
f_{x} = 2x-2 \quad f_{y} = 2y
$$

and setting them to zero

$$
\begin{align}
f_{x} = 0 \to x = 1 \\ \\
f_{y} = 0 \to y = 0
\end{align}
$$

So $(1, 0)$ is a critical point. We take $f_{xx}, f_{xy}, f_{yy}$ to take the Second Derivatives Test.

$$
\begin{align}
f_{xx} = 2 \quad f_{xy} = 0 \quad f_{yy} = 2 \\ \\
D(1,0) = 2(2) - 0^{2} = 4 > 0
\end{align}
$$

And since $f_{xx} > 0$, then $f(1,0) = -1$ is a local minimum.

Now to evaluate $f$ at the boundaries of $D$, we need to draw $D$ on the $xy$-plane. 

![[Pasted image 20260720201330.png]]

We denote the boundaries as $L_{1}, L_{2}, L_{3}$. 

We can describe all the points along $L_{1}$ with the condition $x = 0$ for $-2 \leq y \leq 2$.  If $x = 0$, then $f$ gets to be $f(y) = y^{2}$. It's apparent that $y^{2}$ is at a maximum when $y = \pm 2$ and a minimum at $y = 0$. So the minimum value on $L_{1}$ is $f(0, 0) = 0$ and the maximum value is $f(0, \pm 2) = 4$. 

All points along $L_{2}$ are described along the line $y = x-2$ for $0 \leq x \leq 2$. If $y = x-2$, then substituting this into $f$ gives a function dependent only on $x$:

$$
\begin{align}
f(x) &  = x^{2} + (x-2)^{2} - 2x \\ \\
 & = x^{2} + x^{2} - 4x + 4 - 2x \\ \\
 & = 2x^{2} - 6x + 4 \\ \\
 & = x^{2} - 3x + 2
\end{align}
$$

This is a quadratic and so by taking $f'(x) = 0$ and doing the First Derivative Test, we would see that $f$ has a local minimum at $x = {3}/{2}$. And so $y = -{1}/{2}$. Evaluating $f$ at this point is $f({3}/{2}, -{1}/{2}) = -{1}/{2}$. Evaluating at the vertices $f(2,0) = 0$ and $f(0, -2) = 4$. The max along $L_{2}$ is $f(0,-2) = 4$ and the min is $f({3}/{2}, -{1}/{2}) = -{1}/{2}$.

Points along $L_{3}$ can be described along the line $y = 2 - x$ for $0 \leq x \leq 2$. Substituting this into $f$ gives $f(x) = x^{2} + (2-x)^{2} - 2x$. Taking $f'(x) = 0$,

$$
f'(x) = 2x - 4 = 0 \to x = 2
$$

For values of $x \in [0, 2)$, $f' < 0$ which means $f$ is strictly decreasing along $L_{3}$. The max on $L_{3}$ would then be $f(0,2) = 4$ and the minimum $f(2,0) = 0$. 

Comparing these values against one another, we find that the absolute minimum of $f$ is $f(1,0) = -1$ and the absolute maximum of $f$ is $f(0, \pm 2) = 4$.
