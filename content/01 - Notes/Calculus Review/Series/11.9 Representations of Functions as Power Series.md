
# Representing Functions as Power Series

We are changing our view of what a power series is: The power series sum (expansion) can be a representation of a function on which the interval of convergence makes that representation valid. So for example, we can express $f(x) = \frac{1}{1-x}$ as the sum of the power series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x^{n} = 1 + x + x^{2} + x^{3} + \cdots$ only where $x \in (-1, 1)$. If $x \leq -1$ or  $x \geq 1$, then $f$ cannot be represented by the aforementioned power series.

Using the basic geometric series, we can represent other functions as a power series, granted that $x$ lies on the interval of convergence. 

**Problem:** Find a power series representation for the function and determine the interval of convergence.

$$
f(x) = \frac{2}{3-x}
$$

**Approach:** We can rearrange $f(x)$ to look like the original geometric series $\frac{1}{1-x}$. 

$$
f(x) = \frac{2}{3-x} = \frac{2}{3} \left[ \frac{1}{1-\left( \frac{1}{3}x \right)} \right] 
$$

So our $r = \frac{1}{3}x$ and so the following power series representation is valid if $\left| \frac{1}{3}x \right| < 1 \to |x| < 3$.

$$
f(x) = \frac{2}{3}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left( \frac{1}{3}x \right)^{n} = \frac{2}{3} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^{n}}{3^{n}} \quad \text{if} \quad |x| < 3
$$

---

**Problem:** Find a power series representation for the function and determine the interval of convergence.

$$
f(x) = \frac{x^{2}}{x^{4} + 16}
$$

**Approach:** Rearranging $f(x)$ to see that the geometric series is present, we find

$$
f(x) = \frac{x^{2}}{x^{4} + 16} = \frac{x^{2}}{16} \underbrace{ \left[ \frac{1}{1 - \left( -\frac{x^{4}}{16} \right)} \right]  }_{ \text{Call this}\ g(x) }
$$

where $r = -\frac{x^{4}}{16}$ (this is what we raise to the $n$ in the power series). We can express $g(x)$ as a power series on the interval where

$$
\left| - \frac{x^{4}}{16} \right| < 1 \to |x|^{4} < 16 \to |x| < 2
$$

The power-series expansion for $f$ is 

$$
f(x) =  \frac{x^{2}}{16} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left( -\frac{x^{4}}{16} \right) ^{n} = \frac{x^{2}}{16}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^{n} \frac{x^{4n}}{16^{n}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^{n} \frac{x^{4n+2}}{16^{n+1}}
$$

where the interval of convergence is $(-2, 2)$.

## Derivatives and Integrals of Power Series

We can also leverage the fact that certain functions may be derivatives or antiderivatives of other functions that can be represented as a power series. Therefore, we can differentiate or integrate the power series terms to then represent functions we're interested in. This can be summarized by the following:

> [!theorem]
> If the power series $\sum c_{n}(x-a)^{n}$ has radius of convergence $R > 0$, then the function $f$ defined by
> 
> $$ f(x) = c_{0} + c_{1}(x-a) + c_{2}(x-a)^{2} + \cdots = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_{n}(x-a)^{n} $$
> 
> is differentiable (and thereby continuous) on the interval $(a-R, a+R)$. 
> 
> Moreover, 
> 
> 1. The derivative of $f$ is defined as follows 
> 
> $$ f'(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} nc_{n}x^{n-1} = c_{1} + 2c_{2}(x-a) + 3c_{3}(x-a)^{2} + \cdots \\ \\ $$
> 
> 2. The antiderivative of $f$ is defined as follows
> 
> $$  \int f(x)\, dx = C + \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_{n}\frac{(x-a)^{n+1}}{n+1} = c_{0}(x-a) + \frac{c_{1}}{2}(x-a)^{2} + \frac{c_{2}}{3}(x-a)^{3} + \cdots + C $$
> 
> where $C$ is the constant of integration. 
> 
> The radius of convergence for both power series which represent $f'(x)$ and $\int f(x)\, dx$ is $R$. 

> [!note]
> Every time we take the derivative of a power series we must shift the lower bound for the power series from $n=0$ to $n=1$ since the constant term $c_{0}$ gets zero'd out. Furthermore, we are able to maintain the convention of $\sum c_{n}(x-a)^{n}$ for $\int f(x)\, dx$ if we let $C = C_{0} - ac_{0}$.

The theorem asserts the idea that the derivative of an infinite sum of polynomial terms is the infinite sum of derivatives of polynomial terms; likewise, the integral of an infinite sum of polynomial terms is the infinite sum of integrated polynomial terms. That is, 

$$
\begin{align}
 & \frac{d}{dx} \left[ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_{n}(x-a)^{n} \right] = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{d}{dx} \left[ c_{n}(x-a)^{n} \right] = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} nc_{n}(x-a)^{n-1} \\ \\
 & \int \left[ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_{n}(x-a)^{n} \right] \, dx = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \int \left[ c_{n}(x-a)^{n} \right] \, dx =  C + \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_{n} \frac{(x-a)^{n+1}}{n+1}
\end{align}
$$

> The constant of integration $C$ is the culmination of other constants of integration, that is, $C_{0} = C_{1} + C_{2} + C_{3} + \cdots$


> [!note]
> While the theorem states that the radius of convergence $R$ stays the same throughout the derivative and antiderivative of a power-series, the interval of convergence may differ as endpoints may converge or diverge for a derivative/antiderivative. 

---

**Problem:** 

*Part (a):* Use differentiation to find a power series representation for

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{(1+x)^{2}}
$$

*Part (b):* Then use the result of *part (a)* to find a power series for 

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{(1+x)^{3}}
$$

*Part (c):* Then use *part (b)* to find a power series for 

$$
f(x) = \frac{x^{2}}{(1+x)^{3}}
$$

**Approach:** 

*For part (a)*:

Let us define $\displaystyle g(x) = - \frac{1}{1+x}$, we can use some algebra to see this as the sum of a geometric series. 

$$
g(x) = \frac{1}{1+x} = - \left[ \frac{1}{1-(-x)} \right] 
$$

so $r = -x$ and furthermore

$$
g(x) = - \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^{n}x^{n} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^{n+1}x^{n} \quad |x| < 1
$$

Differentiating $g$ we have

$$
g'(x) = \frac{1}{(1+x)^{2}}
$$

We recognize $f(x) = g'(x)$. Hence, we can represent $f$ as a power series by differentiating the power series that represents $g$. 

$$
g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx} \left[ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^{n-1}nx^{n} \right]  = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{n-1}nx^{n-1}
$$

We can reshift the bounds as to see $f(x)$ as its own power-series independent of $g$. If we let $k = n-1$, then the power series for $f(x)$ can be rewritten as

$$
f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (-1)^{k}(k+1)x^{k} \quad \text{if} \quad|x| < 1 
$$

The radius of convergence remains the same and endpoints diverge so we are able to assert the statement that the interval of convergence for $f(x) = g'(x)$ is still the same as for $g(x)$. 

*Part (b)*:

If we take $g''(x)$, we find

$$
g''(x) = -\frac{2}{(1+x)^{3}}
$$

So in relation to $g''(x)$, the function $f(x) = {1}/{(1+x)^{3}}$ is seen to be $f(x) = -\frac{1}{2}g''(x)$. 

Since $g$ can be represented as a power series, so can $g'$ which follows $g''$ as well. That is, the power series for $g''$ is the derivative of the power series for $g'$. 

$$
\begin{align}
f(x) &  = -\frac{1}{2} \frac{d}{dx} \left[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{n-1}x^{n-1} \right] \\ \\ & = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} (-1)^{n-1}n(n-1)x^{n-2} \\ \\
 & = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} (-1)^{n} n(n-1)x^{n-2}
\end{align}
$$

If we replace let $k = n-2$, then $n = k+2$ and $(-1)^{n} \to (-1)^{k+2} = (-1)^{k}$. So

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}(k+1)(k+2)x^{k} \quad \text{if} \quad|x| < 1 
$$

*For part (c)*:

It's pretty easy to see that the new function $f$ is just another version of the previous function from *part (b)*, call it $f_{0}$. That is, $f(x) = x^{2}f_{0}(x)$. So,

$$
f(x) = \frac{x^{2}}{2} \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} (-1)^{n} n(n-1)x^{n-2} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} (-1)^{n}n(n-1)x^{n}
$$

where the power series representation of $f$ is valid for $|x| < 1$.

The beauty of integrating a power series representation of a function is that it helps us find what the antiderivative may look like or even evaluating definite integrals when integrating the straight function would be too difficult.

**Problem:** Evaluate the indefinite integral as a power series. What is the radius of convergence?

$$
\int x^{2}\ln(1+x)\, dx
$$

**Approach:** We want to first find a power series representation for $x^{2}\ln(1+x)$ and then integrate that power series. First recognize that 

$$
\frac{d}{dx} \left[ \ln(1+x) \right] = \frac{1}{1+x} = \frac{1}{1-(-x)}
$$

which can be represented as the power series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}x^{n}$ whenever $|x| < 1$. So to get the power series representation of $\ln(1+x)$, we can do 

$$
\int \left[ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^{n}x^{n} \right] \, dx = C + \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n}}{n+1}x^{n+1}
$$

If we set $x=0$, then all the $x$ terms wipe out and subsequently the entire power series, but $\ln(1+0) = 0$. So the above power series represents the function $\ln(1+x)$ when $C = 0$. 

Thus,

$$
x^{2}\ln(1+x) = x^{2} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n}}{n+1}x^{n+1} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n}}{n+1}x^{n+3}
$$

That's just the integrand. To find the antiderivative, we integrate the power series for $x^{2}\ln(1+x)$.

$$
\int x^{2}\ln(1+x)\, dx = \int \left[ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n}}{n+1}x^{n+3} \right] \, dx = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n}}{(n+1)(n+4)}x^{n+4}
$$

The power series representation of the antiderivative for $x^{2}\ln(1+x)$ is only valid for $|x| < 1$, since the radius of convergence does not change. 

> We can only choose bounds of integration in power series estimations for $\int_{a}^{b} f(x)\, dx$ when $a$ and $b$ lie in the interval of convergence.

Suppose we wanted to find $\int_{0}^{0.5} x^{2}\ln(1+x) \, dx$. We can estimate the value of $F(x) = \int x^{2}\ln(1+x)$ for a few terms the series representation and then take the difference between $F(0.5)$ and $F(0)$ by FTC. 

If we let $n = 10$, then 

$$
\begin{align}
 & F(0.5) \approx 0.0177254193154 \\ \\
 & F(0) \approx 0
\end{align}
$$

So,

$$
\int_{0}^{0.5} x^{2}\ln(1+x)\, dx \approx 0.0177254193154
$$

By the Alternating Series Estimation Theorem, we can estimate the error to be within

$$
R_{n} \leq |a_{n+1}| = \left| \frac{(-1)^{11}}{12 \cdot 15}(0.5)^{14} \right| = \frac{(0.5)^{14} }{12 \cdot 15} = 3.39\times10^{-7}
$$

since we took $n = 10$.

So this estimation is absolutely guaranteed to be accurate to at least 6 decimal places.
