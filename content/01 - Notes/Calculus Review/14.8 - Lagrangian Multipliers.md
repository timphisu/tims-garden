# Lagrangian Multipliers

## Intuition

### In 2 Dimensions

The usual optimization problem is written as

$$
\begin{align}
 & \max_{x,y} f(x,y) \\ \\
 & \text{subject to}\ g(x,y) = k
\end{align}
$$

where $f$ is called the objective function and the equation $g(x,y) = k$ is called the constraint. 

This problem is asking us to find the maximum value of $f$ given the decision variables $x$ and $y$ satisfy the constraint equation $g(x, y) = k$. Geometrically speaking, we are finding the highest point of $f$ where $(x,y)$ lies on the level curve $g(x,y) = k$. 

Below is the graph of the function $f(x,y) = x^{2} + y^{2} - xy - y$ whose surface is purple. The constraint in this situation is $x^{2} + y^{2} = 4$, whose level curve is the red circle on the $xy$-plane, however when projected on the surface of $f$ gives us a black curve. When maximizing $f$ subject to a constraint, we are finding the highest point on this curve. 

![[Pasted image 20260724151631.png]]

Without having to parameterize the constraint onto $f$, we can look down on the $xy$-plane and compare the level curves of $f$ with the level curve $g(x,y) = k$. As you can see, some of the red level curves of $f$ intersect the green curve, the constraint $x^{2} + y^{2} = 4$. But we're looking for the highest value $c$ such that the level curve $f(x,y) = c$ intersects the level curve of $g(x,y) = k$. That is done by the blue level curve, and the point $(x_{0}, y_{0})$ where the two curves intersect is exactly where the constrained maximum of $f$ lives. Observe that this blue level curve just *touches* the constraint level curve and so it could be argued they share a common tangent line at $(x_{0},y_{0})$.


![[Pasted image 20260724164359.png|400x400]]

Because of that, it's intuitive that their normal lines (line perpendicular to the tangent line) are identical. We can describe the direction of the line normal to the level curve at $(x_{0},y_{0})$ with the gradient vectors of $f$ and $g$. 

> [!excerpt]
> The gradient vector is always perpendicular to the tangent line of a level curve because moving along the curve produces zero change in the function value, meaning the steepest ascent points straight across it at a right angle.

That means we can write $\nabla f(x_{0}, y_{0}) = \lambda \nabla g(x_{0}, y_{0})$ where $\lambda$ is some scalar. 

We can go down the same path of logic for finding the minimum since the same geometry applies when finding the smallest value of $c$ such that the level curves $f(x,y) = c$ and $g(x,y) = k$ intersect and that corresponds to finding the lowest point on the curve $C$.

![[Pasted image 20260724174034.png|400x400]]

### In 3 Dimensions

The idea works also for optimizing $f(x,y,z)$ subject to $g(x,y,z) = k$. Instead of level curves, we're working with level surfaces. Below, the sphere could be a level surface of the squared distance function $d^{2} = (x-a)^{2} + (y-b)^{2} + (z-c)^{2}$. There are plenty of radii to choose from for our level surface $f(x,y,z) = c$ that intersects the plane $g(x,y,z) = k$ but the point $P$ that minimizes the distance is one where the sphere just *touches* the plane. We know that the gradient vector will always be perpendicular to its level surface. That means $\nabla f(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}) = \lambda \nabla g(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})$ where $\lambda$ is a scalar called a **Lagrangian multiplier**.

![[Pasted image 20260724193902.png]]

Suppose $f$ has an extreme value at point $P(x_{0}, y_{0}, z_{0})$ on the surface $S$ and let $C$ be a curve with vector equation $\vec{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), z(t) \rangle$ that lies on $S$ and passes through $P$. If $t_{0}$ is the parameter value corresponding to the point $P$, then $\vec{r}(t_{0})) = \langle x_{0}, y_{0}, z_{0} \rangle$. Let $h = f(\vec{r}(t))$ so that the composite function $h(t) = (f(x(t), y(t), z(t))$ represents the values of $f$ taken on by the curve $C$. Since $f$ has an extreme value at $P$, $h$ has an extreme value at $t_{0}$, so $h'(t_{0}) = 0$. 

If $f$ is differentiable, then by the Chain Rule,

$$
\begin{align}
0 & = h'(t_{0}) \\ \\
 & = f_{x}(x_{0}, y_{0}, z_{0})x'(t_{0}) + f_{y}(x_{0}, y_{0}, z_{0})y'(t_{0}) + f_{z}(x_{0}, y_{0}, z_{0})z'(t_{0}) \\ \\
 & = \nabla f(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}) \cdot \vec{r}\ '(t_{0})
\end{align}
$$

Hence the gradient vector of $f$ at $P$ is orthogonal to the tangential vector $\vec{r}\ '(t)$ on every curve $C$ which passes through $P$.

## Method of Lagrangian Multipliers

> [!theorem]
> To find the maximum and minimum values of $f(x,y,z)$ such that the constraint $g(x,y,z) = k$ (assuming such extreme values exist and $\nabla g \ne \vec{0}$ on the surface $g(x,y,z) = k$:
> 
> 1. Find all values $x,y,z$ and $\lambda$ such that 
> 
> $$ \begin{aligned} \nabla f(x,y,z) &  = \lambda \nabla g(x,y,z) \\ \\ g(x,y,z) &  = k\end{aligned} $$
> 
> 2. Evaluate $f$ at all the points $(x,y,z)$ from the first step. The largest of these is the maximum constrained value of $f$ and the smallest is the minimum constrained value.

---

**Problem:** Use the method of Lagrangian multipliers to find the extreme values of $f(x,y) = x^{2} + y^{2} - xy - y$ subject to the constraint $x^{2} + y^{2} = 4$. 

**Approach:** Recognizing $x^{2} + y^{2} = 4$ as $g(x,y,z) = k$ and so by taking the gradient vectors of $f$ and $g$, we find

$$
\begin{align}
f_{x} = 2x - y
\end{align}
$$



> [!note] Possibility of Local Extrema Being a Solution
> If $x = x_{0}, y = y_{0}, z = z_{0}$ is a solution to this system of equations and $\lambda \ne 0$, then $\nabla f(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})$ is parallel to $\nabla g(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})$ as before. But if $\lambda = 0$, then $\nabla f(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}) = \vec{0}$ so $(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})$ is a critical point of $f$ which makes it worth exploring whether $f(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})$ is an extreme value on its domain, and if so could also be an extreme value of $f$ subject to $g(x,y,z) = k$. 

## Two Constraints

Suppose we want to find minimum and maximum values of a function $f(x,y,z)$ subject to two constraints, also called **side conditions**, of the form $g(x,y,z) = k$ and $h(x,y,z) = c$. The former geometry will apply in a similar way. Suppose $f$ has that extreme value at $P(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})$. Let $C$ be the intersecting curve between the level surfaces of $g$ and $h$, described by the vector $\vec{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), z(t) \rangle$. $P$ will have $\nabla g$ and $\nabla h$ orthogonal to the curve. Since $\nabla f$ is also orthogonal to the curve at $P$, then the gradient vector of $f$ is in the span of $\nabla g$ and $\nabla h$, assuming the vectors are nonzero and are not parallel. Then

$$
\nabla f(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}) = \lambda \nabla g(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}) + \mu \nabla h(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
$$

for some scalars $\lambda$ and $\mu$ called *Lagrangian multipliers*.

To see this in action, consider the graph below. 

![[Pasted image 20260725104004.png|500x400]]

Above shows the level surface of $x^{2} + y^{2} + z^{2} = 5$, which follows the form $f(x,y,z) = m$ where $m=5$ is the constrained maximum of $f$.  $A$, the point where the extreme value lives, is exactly where the sphere just *kisses* the hyperbola $y^{2} - z^{2} = 1$ and the plane $x-y = 1$ simultaneously. 

![[Pasted image 20260725190921.png]]

From a different perspective, if the hyperbolic level surface is seen as $g(x,y,z) = k$ and the plane as $h(x,y,z) = c$, then we see that the vector on the left $\nabla g$ and the vector on the right $\nabla h$ are orthogonal to the green curve $C$ and span a plane for which the vector in the middle $\nabla f$ is part of that plane. Hence $\nabla f$ can be expressed as a linear combination of $\nabla g$ and $\nabla h$. 

## The Lagrangian

Suppose the method of Lagrangian multipliers involves solving the system

$$
\begin{align}
 & \nabla f(x,y,z) = \lambda \nabla g(x,y,z) + \mu \nabla h(x,y,z) \\ \\
 & g(x,y,z) = k \\ \\
 & h(x,y,z) = c
\end{align}
$$

We can repackage the system of equations into a single construct called the **Lagrangian**, written as,

$$
\mathcal{L}(x,y,z,\lambda,\mu) = f(x,y,z) - \lambda[g(x,y,z) - k] - \mu \left[ h(x,y,z) - c \right] 
$$

There's nothing meaningful from the values of $\mathcal{L}$ but when we set $\nabla \mathcal{L} = \vec{0}$, we're setting each component of $\nabla \mathcal{L}$ to zero,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{x} = 0, \mathcal{L}_{y} = 0, \mathcal{L}_{z} = 0, \mathcal{L}_{\lambda} = 0, \mathcal{L}_{\mu} = 0
$$

Then what we see is,

$$
\begin{align}
 & \mathcal{L}_{x} = 0 \to f_{x} - \lambda g_{x} - \mu h_{x} = 0 \to f_{x} = \lambda g_{x} + \mu h_{x} \\ \\
 & \mathcal{L}_{y} = 0 \to f_{y} - \lambda g_{y} - \mu h_{y} = 0 \to f_{y} = \lambda g_{y} + \mu h_{y} \\ \\
 & \mathcal{L}_{z} = 0 \to f_{z} - \lambda g_{z} - \mu h_{z} = 0 \to f_{z} = \lambda g_{z} + \mu h_{z} \\ \\
 & \mathcal{L}_{\lambda} = 0 \to g(x,y,z) - k = 0 \to g(x,y,z) = k \\ \\
 & \mathcal{L}_{\mu} = 0 \to h(x,y,z) - c = 0 \to h(x,y,z) = c
\end{align}
$$

So finding the critical points of the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}$ is equivalent to solving the system of equations for the method of Lagrangian multipliers.
