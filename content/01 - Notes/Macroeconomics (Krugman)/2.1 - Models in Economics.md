
Models are a simplified representation of reality that helps explain dynamics found in the real world; moreover, they rely on the assumption of *ceteris paribus*, or "holding other things constant". 

# Production Possibility Frontier

This is a model that simplifies an economy to producing two kinds of goods, such that it can be graphed on a 2D plane. It illustrates the trade off between producing the max quantity of one good for any quantity of another (and vise versa). 

Suppose an economy produced two goods: bundles of what and guns. We could graph all the possible combinations of quantities this economy could produce. with quantity of wheat on the $y$-axis and quantity of ammunition on the $x$-axis.

![[Pasted image 20260627205201.png]]

The boundary line of this graph is the production possibilities frontier. An economy producing combinations, such as points $A$ and $B$, on this line are being *efficient in production*, that is, there are no missed opportunities to produce something else. Going down this line, we cannot produce any more of say guns, without giving up the production of another good like wheat. All points below the PPF curve like $C$ is feasible and is within our production capabilities but is inefficient since we can produce more wheat without producing less guns. A point like $D$ is outside our PPF, so therefore is not feasible. 

## Efficiency

This model illustrates efficiency in production, however efficiency as a whole is a 3-part game where it also requires efficiency in allocation, that is, making consumers well off as possible. Suppose it is in society's best interest, say we're in war for example, to produce more guns than wheat: Then a point like $B$ is more preferred than point $A$, all while both points are efficient. The third part of efficiency involves delivering the right goods to the right people. What if the economy was producing the efficient combination of wheat and guns that made society better off; however, guns were not being properly delivered to soldiers on the battlefront or wheat wasn't being stocked in supermarkets. That would not be an efficient delivery of goods. 

## Opportunity Cost

Opportunity cost is illustrated in this model since if we move our production from $A$ to $B$, we gain say 30 more guns in production but forgo 60 bundles of wheat. That means for every 30 guns the economy produces, 60 bundles of wheat is lost, that is one bundle wheat forgoes $\frac{60}{30} = 2$ guns. In fact, this opportunity cost ratio is the slope of this curve. The straight line of the PPF assumes that opportunity cost remains constant no matter how much quantity is produced. 

We can have a bend in our PPF curve which illustrates the idea that opportunity cost is rising as the quantity produced increases. 

![[Pasted image 20260627212442.png]]

Look at the example two-good economy above: At zero phones produced, the maximum quantity of laptops the economy can produce is 18. Making 20 phones only requires to forgo 3 laptops; however, if we increase our production of phones by +5, we dramatically drop our production of laptops to 5. Why could that be? Well, this illustrates the idea that the economy will use its best-suited resources for a good, making the production of a few units relatively cheap. But as we ramp up production, less adaptable resources are then used instead that dramatically decreases the amount used for other goods. 

## Economic Growth

*Economic growth* is defined as the growing ability of the economy to produce goods and services. Another way of saying that is the capability of producing more "stuff". We can illustrate this idea by the shifting of the production possibility frontier outward. 

![[Pasted image 20260627220901.png]]

Economic growth comes from two sources 

1. An increase in factors of production
2. Use of technology

Factors of production include land, labor, physical capital (buildings and machines), and human capital (education and skills). These all represent broad categories as the usefulness of land is different between say Nebraska and Florida. Factors of production is something used to create a good or service but is not used up in the process of production: For example, skilled workers use machines to produce cars but they do not get used up the way sheet metal does.

The use of technology can improve the rate of production while having the same level of factors of production given. 

## Comparative Advantage

One country may be better at producing one type of good than another country but be relatively worse at producing another which the other country is better at. This is the idea of *comparative advantage*. 

Suppose the United States and Brazil were both two-good economies and produced large airplanes and small jets. Their production possibility frontiers are graphed as follows:

![[Pasted image 20260628113237.png]]

At most, the US can produce 30 large jets and at most 40 small jets, so the opportunity cost of 1 small jet is equal to $\frac{3}{4}$ of a large jet. For Brazil, they can produce at most 10 large jets and at most 30 small jets, which means their opportunity cost of a small jet is $\frac{1}{3}$ of a large jet. Since $\frac{1}{3} < \frac{3}{4}$, Brazil has a comparative advantage over the United States in producing large jets. On the other hand, if the ratios of opportunity cost were flipped, we'd find it takes the United States to give up $\frac{4}{3}$ small jets for one large jet while it takes Brazil $3$ small jets. So the United States has a comparative advantage over Brazil in producing large jets. 

Both countries should produce what they have a comparative advantage in. Moreover, they can benefit from trading from one another and "consume" more planes than they could've when not trading. As you can see in the graph below, the United States decides to no longer produce any small jets and just produce their max output of large jets, and Brazil doesn't produce any large jets anymore just small jets. But in the mix, Brazil and the United States strike up a deal where the United States trades 10 large jets in return for 20 small jets: that's a trade ratio of one large jet for every 2 small jets, that is, $\frac{10}{20} = \frac{1}{2}$. Why is that? Well, the US is willing to trade at a ratio that's less than their opportunity cost ratio back at home $\frac{3}{4}$; otherwise, it's just better to stick producing and consuming their own large and small jets. 

![[Pasted image 20260628122004.png]]

Think about it like making coffee at home during a work day and instead of dollar amounts we thought about it as the opportunity cost of eating out during your lunch break (assuming that's the only opportunity cost other than time and energy). You could make coffee at home for $\$2$ or you could have coffee at Dunkin' on your way to work for $\$5$, and suppose the price of a meal on your lunch break at say Chic fil' A is $\$10$: So the price of coffee made at home in "lunch amounts" is $\frac{2}{10} = \frac{1}{5}$ and having coffee outside is $\frac{5}{10} = \frac{1}{2}$. I'd have to make 5 coffees at home to forgo only one day I don't eat out at Chic fil' A, but if I go to Dunkin' twice that week or even buy a second coffee for a friend, I can't go to Chic fil' A for a day. Another way to think about this opportunity cost ratio is if I went to Dunkin' every day before work for two weeks (two work weeks = 10 days), I'd have to give up a whole work week of not eating Chic fil' A. So I'm better off making coffee at home to have more "opportunity" to go to Chic fil' A during my lunch break. Suppose Dunkin' had an anniversary discount that made coffee onlybe $\$1$, then it'd make more sense to get coffee before work!

Now back to the jet example, the United States can produce more of both large jets and small jets on their own compared to Brazil at perhaps a more efficient rate, which is called an **absolute advantage**. Despite that, the *opportunity cost*, or the cost of making a large jet at home is more than just having small jets be made by your trading partner.

# Check Your Understanding

True or false? Explain your answer

1. An increase in the amount of resources available to Boeing for use in producing Dreamliners and small jets does not change its production possibility frontier.

This is not true as increasing the resources available to Boeing for use in production would yield an increase in production of "everything" or "more stuff", that is Boeing would use the new added resources to their benefit in order to produce more jets of both kinds. This would be illustrated by an outward shift in the production possibility frontier.

2. A technological change that allows Boeing to build more small jets for any amount of Dreamliners built results in a change in its production possibility frontier.

True. A technological change in Boeing's production would make building more small jets for any amount of Dreamliners means production is more efficient, that is, the opportunity cost to build small jets (in return for Dreamliners) decreases. We would see a change in the curve of the production possibility frontier. 

3. The production possibility frontier is useful because it illustrates how much of one good an economy must give up to get more of another good regardless of whether resources are being used efficiently.

This is not entirely true. To see the relationship between what you must forgo in order to gain something, you must be tracing along the boundary curve of the production possibility frontier. When your production mix is below the PPF curve, which is an inefficient use of resources, you can produce more of one good without having to give up another good.

In Italy, an automobile can be produced by 8 workers in one day and a washing machine by 3 workers in one day. In the United States, an automobile can be produced by 6 workers in one day and a washing machine by 2 workers in one day.

1. Which country has an absolute advantage in the production of automobiles? In washing machines?

The United States has an absolute advantage in the production of automobiles and washing machines because they can produce a single unit with less workers, which means they are more productive. 

2. Which country has a comparative advantage in the production of washing machines? In automobiles?

However, if we take a look at how many automobile workers Italy must forgo to produce a washing machine, the opportunity cost is $\frac{3}{8}$. On the other hand, the US must forgo 6 automobile workers to make 2 washing machines or $\frac{6}{2} = 3$. So Italy has a comparative advantage in making washing machines. Inverting this ratio to find the opportunity cost of producing automobiles, the US's opportunity cost is $\frac{1}{3} < \frac{3}{8}$, so the United States has a comparative advantage of making automobiles. 

3. What pattern of specialization results in the greatest gains from trade between the two countries?

If the United States specializes in just making automobiles and Italy in making washing machines, they make the greatest gains in trade.

Use the circular-flow diagram to explain how an increase in the amount of money spent by households results in an increase in the number of jobs in the economy. Describe in words what the circular-flow diagram predicts.

When households spend more money, they are flowing money into the market for goods and services. In order to keep up with this demand, shops hire more people on the front-end like sales clerks (for example Target hires seasonal workers in expectation of the holiday demand) and if there's enough money long-term that there's more orders coming from firms, then firms will also hire more employees to deal with this new demand (for example, the Korean firm that makes Buddak ramen, sold at Target, has long-term demand and will hire workers in logistics so that the supply chain remains stable and they're able to keep up).
