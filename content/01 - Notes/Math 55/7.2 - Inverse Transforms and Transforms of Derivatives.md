
# Inverse Laplace Transforms

If $\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} = F(s)$ is the *Laplace transform*, then we say $f(t)$ is the **inverse Laplace transform** of $F(s)$, which is written as $f(t)  = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s)\}$.

It's also helpful to know that the inverse Laplace transform also holds linearity properties, that is, for constants $\alpha$ and $\beta$,

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{\alpha F(s) + \beta G(s)\} = \alpha f(t) + \beta g(t)
$$

* where $F(s)$ and $G(s)$ are Laplace transforms of $f(t)$ and $g(t)$ respectively

We can take the previous theorem of known Laplace transforms and reverse them to create inverse formulas.
## Some Inverse Transforms

$$
\begin{align}
 & \mathbf{(a)} & \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{s} \right\} = 1 \\ \\
 & \mathbf{(b)} & \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{n!}{s^{n+1}} \right \} = t^{n} \quad \text{for}\ n = 1, 2, 3, ... \\ \\
 & \mathbf{(c)} & \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{s-a}\right\} = e^{ at } \\ \\
 & \mathbf{(d)} & \mathcal{L}^{-1} \left\{ \frac{s}{s^{2} + k^{2}} \right\} = \sin kt \\ \\
 & \mathbf{(e)} & \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{k}{s^{2} + k^{2}}\right\} = \cos kt
\end{align}
$$


**Problem:** Evaluate $\displaystyle \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{s^{4}} \right\}$

**Approach:** For $F(s) = {1}/{s^{4}}$, we recognize this as (b) from the above formulas where $n = 3$. However, we're missing a constant of $n!$ or $3!$, and just like integration, we account for this by multiplying the inverse scalar, that is,

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{s^{4}} \right\} = \frac{1}{3!} \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{3!}{s^{3+1}} \right\} = \frac{t^{3}}{3!}
$$

---

**Problem:**  Evaluate $\displaystyle \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{10s}{s^{2} + 16} \right\}$

**Approach:** We recognize $k = 4$ which follows the formula (d)

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{10s}{s^{2} + 16} \right\} = 10 \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{s}{s^{2} + 4^{2}}\right\} = 10\cos 4t
$$

---

> Since $k$ doesn't have to be an integer, we can get inverse transforms like the following

**Problem:** Evaluate $\displaystyle \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{s + 1}{s^{2} + 2} \right\}$

**Approach:** By linearity of the Laplace inverse transform, we can split this into distinct inverse transforms

$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{s+1}{s^{2} + 2} \right\} &  = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{s}{s^{2} + 2} \right\} + \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{s^{2} + 2} \right\} & \text{Linearity of } \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s)\} \\ \\
 & = \cos \sqrt{ 2 }t + \frac{1}{\sqrt{ 2 }} \sin \sqrt{ 2 }t  & \text{Formulas (d)  and (e)} 
\end{align}
$$

---

**Problem:** Evaluate $\displaystyle \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{s+1}{s^{2}-4s} \right\}$ 

**Approach:** It's not necessarily apparent to us that $F(s)$ fits one of the previously known formulas, however if we split the expression via. partial fraction decomposition, we find

$$
\frac{s+1}{s(s-4)} = \frac{{5}/{4}}{s-4} - \frac{{1}/{4}}{s}
$$

Hence by linearity of the inverse Laplace transform,

$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{s+1}{s^{2} - 4s} \right\} &  = \frac{5}{4} \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{s-4} \right\} - \frac{1}{4} \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{s} \right\} \\ \\
 & = \frac{5}{4}e^{ 4t } - \frac{1}{4}
\end{align}
$$
---

**Problem:** Evaluate $\displaystyle \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{s^{2} + s - 72} \right\}$

**Approach:** Since $F(s)$ is a rational function, we either recognize it as one of the $\sin kt$/$\cos kt$ forms or split up the fraction with partial fraction decomposition. By seeing $-72$, something tells me that the polynomial $s^{2} + s - 72$ has roots and by the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra can be rewritten as a product of linear factors. By doing some side work, we come to find $s = 9, -8$. 

And then doing partial fraction decomposition, 

$$
F(s) = \frac{1}{s^{2} + s - 72} = \frac{1}{(s-9)(s+8)} = \frac{A}{s-9} + \frac{B}{s+8}
$$

Subsequently after multiplying by the LCD,

$$
\begin{align}
 & A(s+8) + B(s-9) = 1 \\ \\ \\
 & \text{Let}\ s = 9 : &  A(9 + 8) = 1 \to A = \frac{1}{17} \\ \\
 & \text{Let}\ s= -8 : & B(-8-9) = 1 \to B = -\frac{1}{17}
\end{align}
$$

which by the linearity of the inverse Laplace gives us

$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{s^{2} + s -72} \right\} &  = \frac{1}{17} \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{s-9} \right\} - \frac{1}{17} \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{s+8} \right\} \\ \\
 & = \frac{1}{17}e^{ 9t }- \frac{1}{17}e^{ -8t }
\end{align}
$$

## Transforms of Derivatives

In order to use Laplace transforms (and their inverses) to solve differential equations, we need to know how to transform derivatives $f'(t)$ and $f''(t)$ and so on. 

> [!note]
> We assume here that $f(t), f'(t), f''(t), ..., f^{(n)}(t)$ are of exponential order

Consider the following

$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}\{f'(t)\} &  = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }f'(t) \, dt = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st } \, df(t)  & \text{Integral definition} \\ \\
 & = e^{ -st }f(t) \Big|_{t=0}^{t=\infty} - \int_{0}^{\infty} f(t)\, de^{ -st }  & \text{Integration by parts} \\ \\
 & = (0 - f(0)) + s\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }f(t) \, dt \\ \\
 & = -f(0) + sF(s) & \text{Recognizing transform}\ F(s) \\ \\
\end{align}
$$

And hence,

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{f'(t)\} = sF(s) - f(0)
$$

---

We can do the same thing for $\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{f''(t)\}$

$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{f''(t)\} &  = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }f''(t) \, dt = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st } \, df'(t) \\ \\
 & = e^{ -st }f'(t) \Big|_{t=0}^{t=\infty} - \int_{0}^{\infty} f'(t) \, de^{ -st } \\ \\
 & = -f'(0) + s \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }f'(t) \, dt =  -f'(0) + s \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st } \, df(t) \\ \\
 & = -f'(0) + s \left( e^{ -st }f(t) \Big|_{0}^{\infty} - \int_{0}^{\infty} f(t) \, de^{ -st } \right) \\ \\
 & = -f'(0) + s \left( - f(0) + s \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }f(t)\, dt\right) \\ \\
 & = -f'(0) -sf(0) + s^{2} F(s)
\end{align}
$$

which gives way for the following theorem

> [!theorem]
> If $f, f', ..., f^{(n-1)}$ are continuous on $[0, \infty)$ and are of exponential order and if $f^{(n)}(t)$ is piecewise continuous on $[0, \infty)$, then 
> 
> $$\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{f^{(n)}(t)\} = s^{n}F(s) - s^{n-1}f(0) - s^{n-2}f'(0) - \cdots - f^{(n-1)}(0)$$
> 
> where $F(s) = \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}$

# Solving Linear ODEs with Laplace Transforms

> [!excerpt]
> It is apparent from the result given in the last theorem that $\mathcal{L}\{{d^{n}y}/{dt^{n}}\}$ depends on $Y(s) = \mathcal{L}\{y(t)\}$ and the $n-1$ derivatives of $y(t)$ evaluated at $t = 0$. This property makes the Laplace transform ideally suited for solving linear initial-value problems in which the differential equation has constant coefficients.

## General Process to Solve

1. Take the Laplace transform such that the transformed differential equation becomes an algebraic equation in terms of $s$

2. Rearrange to find the equation explicitly for $Y(s)$ via. algebraic manipulation as well as implementing initial conditions

3. Apply the inverse Laplace transform such that we find the unknown $y(t)$ that satisfies the DE

---

**Problem:** Use the Laplace transform to solve the given initial-value problem.

$$
y' + 5y = e^{ 4t }, \quad y(0) = 2
$$

**Approach:** First step is to apply the Laplace transform to both sides of the DE.

$$
\begin{align}
 & \mathcal{L}\{y'\} + 5\mathcal{L}\{y\} = \mathcal{L}\{e^{ 4t }\} & \text{Linearity of}\ \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} \\ \\
 & sY(s) - y(0) + 5Y(s) = \frac{1}{s - 4} & \text{Laplace of derivatives and}\ e^{ at } \\ \\
 & (s+5)Y(s) - 2 = \frac{1}{s-4} & \text{Initial-condition}\ y(0) = 2
\end{align}
$$

The second step is to solve for $Y(s)$ which by moving over 2 and dividing by $s+5$, we find

$$
Y(s) = \frac{1}{(s-4)(s+5)} + \frac{2}{s+5}
$$

The next step of the process is to prepare the transform $Y(s)$ so we can use known formulas to apply the inverse such that we get $y(t)$. Looking at the first term, we need to do partial fraction decomposition to split this into an easier function(s) to inverse transform. 

So in doing PFD, we need to solve for constants $A, B$

$$
\begin{align}
 & \frac{1}{(s-4)(s+5)} = \frac{A}{s-4} + \frac{B}{s+5} \\ \\
 & A(s+5) + B(s-4) = 1 & \text{Multiply by LCD} \\ \\
 & s = -5 \implies B(-9) = 1 \to B = -\frac{1}{9} \\ \\
 & s = 4 \implies A(9) = 1 \to A = \frac{1}{9} \\ \\
 & \frac{1}{(s-4)(s+5)} = \frac{{1}/{9}}{s-4} - \frac{{1}/{9}}{s+5} 
\end{align}
$$

> It helps to write the fractional constants on the numerator so you don't make the mistake of losing some numbers.

Using this result, 

$$
Y(s) = \frac{1}{9} \left( \frac{1}{s-4} - \frac{1}{s+5} \right) + \frac{2}{s+5}
$$

Lastly, we apply the inverse $\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{Y(s)\}$ using formulas we know for $e^{ at }$.

$$
\begin{align}
y(t) &  = \frac{1}{9}e^{ 4t }- \frac{1}{9}e^{ -5t } + 2e^{ -5t } \\ \\
  & = \frac{1}{9}e^{ 4t } + \frac{17}{9}e^{ -5t }
\end{align}
$$
