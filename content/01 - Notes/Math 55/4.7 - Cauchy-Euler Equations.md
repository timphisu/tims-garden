# Cauchy-Euler Equations

> [!definition]
> The following linear differential equation with variable coefficients is called a **Cauchy-Euler equation**:
> 
> $$
> a_{n}x^{n}\frac{dy^{n}}{dx^{n}} + a_{n-1}x^{n-1}\frac{dy^{n-1}}{dx^{n-1}} + \cdots + a_{1}x\frac{dy}{dx} + a_{0}y(x) = g(x)
> $$
> 
> where each degree of the monomial coefficient $x^{i}$ matches the order of differentiation $y^{(i)}$ for $0 \leq i \leq n$.

## Second-Order Homogeneous Case

Our interest for this section is the second-order case homogeneous DE:

$$
ax^{2}y'' + bxy' + cy = 0
$$

Its solution is going to be of the form $y = x^{m}$ where $m$ is a coefficient that is to be determined. 

After plugging in this solution to our differential equation,

$$
\begin{align}
 & y' = mx^{m-1} \\ & y'' = m(m-1)x^{m-2} \\ \\
\implies & ax^{2}\left[ m(m-1)x^{m-2} \right] + bx \left[ mx^{m-1} \right] + cx^{m} = 0 \\ \\
 & am(m-1)x^{m} + bmx^{m} + cx^{m} = 0 \\ \\
 & x^{m} \left[ am(m-1) + bm + c \right]  = 0
\end{align}
$$

We find the actual solution if we solve for $m$ in the auxiliary equation, rearranged as

$$
am^{2} + (b-a)m + c = 0
$$

The 3 cases of the roots $m_{1}$ and $m_{2}$ yields distinct forms of the solution based on each case.

> [!note]
> The form of solution $y = x^{m}$ does not change for higher-order cases of the Cauchy-Euler equation, nor does the method of solution, only the resulting polynomial after substituting into the homogeneous case as it would no longer be a quadratic. 
### Case I: Distinct Real Roots

Since the roots are distinct (and real), we find two linearly independent solutions $y_{1} = x^{m_{1}}$ and $y = x^{m_{2}}$. 

So the general solution would look like

$$
y = c_{1}x^{m_{1}} + c_{2}x^{m_{2}}
$$

### Case II: Repeated Real Roots

If $m_{1} = m_{2}$, then we run into the issue with only finding one solution $y_{1} = x^{m}$ but after using [[Reduction of Order|reduction of order]], a second solution is found which is of the form $y = x^{m}\ln(x)$.

The general solution is then

$$
y = c_{1}x^{m} + c_{2}x^{m}\ln(x)
$$

---

For higher-order cases, if $m$ is a root of multiplicity $k$, then it can be shown that 

$$
x^{m}, x^{m}\ln(x), x^{m}[\ln(x)]^{2}, ..., x^{m}[\ln(x)]^{k-1}
$$

are linearly independent solutions. The general solution would correspondingly be a linear combination of these solutions.

### Case III: Conjugate Complex Roots

If the roots of the auxiliary equation are the conjugate pair $m_{1} = \alpha + \beta i, m_{2} = \alpha - \beta i$, then the general solution is of the form

$$
y = x^{\alpha} \left[ c_{1}\cos(\beta \ln x) + c_{2}\sin(\beta \ln x) \right] 
$$

which can be found in a similar process that we did when resolving complex roots to the auxiliary equation for [[Homogeneous Linear Equations with Constant Coefficients]]
