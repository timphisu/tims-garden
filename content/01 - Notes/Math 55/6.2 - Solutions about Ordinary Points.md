
Consider the 2nd-order linear homogeneous differential equation:

$$
\begin{align}
 & a_{2}(x)y'' + a_{1}(x)y' + a_{0}(x)y = 0 \\ \\
 & y'' + P(x)y' + Q(x)y = 0  & \leftarrow \text{Std. Form}
\end{align}
$$

We say a point $x = x_{0}$ is an ordinary point if $P$ and $Q$ are analytic at $x_{0}$, that is $P$ and $Q$ can be represented as a power series centered at the point $x_{0}$. Otherwise, $x_{0}$ is said to be a singular point if at least $P$ or $Q$ fails to be analytic at the point. So given a point $x = x_{0}$, it is singular if $a_{2}(x_{0}) = 0$.

> In other words, a point is a "singularity" if it zero's out $y''$ hence making it impossible to represent the solution as a power series. 

> [!theorem]
> If $x = x_{0}$ is an ordinary point of $a_{2}(x)y'' + a_{1}(x)y' + a_{0}(x)y = 0$, then we can always find two linearly independent solutions of the form $y = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}(x - x_{0})^{n}$.
> 

This is similar to other guarantees of $n$ linearly independent solutions for an $n$-th order differential equation—only difference being our solutions are represented as power-series and hence must depend on being analytic at some point.  In our work, our power series will be centered at $x_{0} = 0$.

---

**Problem:** Solve the following DE

$$
y'' - xy = 0
$$

**Approach:** Since there are no singular points, we can use a power-series centered at $x = 0$ (which we expect the series to converge for $|x| < \infty$) to represent our solution.

$$
\text{If}\ y = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^{n} \implies y'' = \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n(n-1)c_{n}x^{n-2}
$$

> The reason why the summation lower bound starts at $n=2$ for $y''$ is because of the terms that get zero'd out in the process of differentiation.

Substituting this into the differential equation, we find

$$
\begin{align}
 & \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n(n-1)c_{n}x^{n-2} - x\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^{n} = 0 \\ \\
 & \underbrace{ \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n(n-1)c_{n}x^{n-2} }_{ k = n-2 } - \underbrace{ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^{n+1} }_{ k= n+1 } = 0
\end{align}
$$

By shifting the index for each power-series, we can bring the two terms together under one summation. We do this by letting $k = n-2$ for the left term and $k = n+1$ for the right term. 

> The choice of $k$ in relation to the index $n_{i}$ has to do with making the exponents match. By making exponents match, we can group coefficients and find interesting patterns as you will see.

* For the left term, letting $k = n-2 \implies n = k+2$ and since the summation starts at $n = 2$, then the lower bound must be $k = 2 -2 = 0$.

* For the right term, letting $k = n+1 \to n = k - 1$ gives us that the summation should start at $k = 0+1 = 1$ since the summation starts at $n = 0$.

Hence, we can rewrite the expression as

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (k+2)(k+1)c_{k+2}x^{k} - \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} c_{k-1}x^{k} = 0
$$

And then by pulling the term where $k=0$ out of the left summation, we shift the index by one, making it possible now to group the power-series together under one summation.

$$
2c_{2} + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[ (k+1)(k+2)c_{k+2} - c_{k-1} \right] x^{k} = 0
$$

Now remember that the RHS of this equation is zero. We could say that the equation is true whenever $x = 0$ but our goal is for this equation to remain true for all $x$ in the interval. Hence by the [[Identity Property of Zero]], we could say that the coefficients of the power-series above are all zero, that is,

$$
\begin{align}
c_{2} = 0 \\ \\
(k+1)(k+2)c_{k+2} - c_{k-1} = 0
\end{align}
$$

Further rewriting one of the above relations gives us a [[Recurrence Relation|recurrence relation]] of

$$
c_{k+2} = \frac{c_{k-1}}{(k+1)(k+2)}, \quad k = 1, 2, 3, ...
$$

> [!tip]
> A general rule of thumb in writing recurrence relations is that we should express the coefficients of higher subscripts in terms of lower subscripts. In this case, express $c_{k+2}$ in terms of $c_{k-1}$. 
> 
> It's also important that for this to be a recurrence relation, we must include what $k$ is; otherwise, there's confusion as to where $k$ starts. That's informed by the lower summation bound of our power-series.
