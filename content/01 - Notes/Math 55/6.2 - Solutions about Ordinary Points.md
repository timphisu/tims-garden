# Power-Series Solutions to DEs about Ordinary Points

Consider the 2nd-order linear homogeneous differential equation:

$$
\begin{align}
 & a_{2}(x)y'' + a_{1}(x)y' + a_{0}(x)y = 0 \\ \\
 & y'' + P(x)y' + Q(x)y = 0  & \leftarrow \text{Std. Form}
\end{align}
$$

* where $a_{2}(x), a_{1}(x), a_{0}(x)$ are <u>polynomials</u>

We say a point $x = x_{0}$ is an ordinary point if $P$ and $Q$ are analytic at $x_{0}$, that is $P$ and $Q$ can be represented as a power series centered at the point $x_{0}$. Otherwise, $x_{0}$ is said to be a singular point if at least $P$ or $Q$ fails to be analytic at the point. So given a point $x = x_{0}$, it is singular if $a_{2}(x_{0}) = 0$.

> In other words, a point is a "singularity" if it zero's out $y''$ hence making it impossible to represent the solution as a power series. 

> [!theorem]
> If $x = x_{0}$ is an ordinary point of $a_{2}(x)y'' + a_{1}(x)y' + a_{0}(x)y = 0$, then we can always find two linearly independent solutions of the form $y = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}(x - x_{0})^{n}$.
> 

This is similar to other guarantees of $n$ linearly independent solutions for an $n$-th order differential equation—only difference being our solutions are represented as power-series and hence must depend on being analytic at some point.  In our work, our power series will be centered at $x_{0} = 0$.

---

**Problem:** Solve the following DE

$$
y'' - xy = 0
$$

**Approach:** Since there are no singular points, we can use a power-series centered at $x = 0$ (which we expect the series to converge for $|x| < \infty$) to represent our solution.

$$
\text{If}\ y = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^{n} \implies y'' = \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n(n-1)c_{n}x^{n-2}
$$

> The reason why the summation lower bound starts at $n=2$ for $y''$ is because of the terms that get zero'd out in the process of differentiation.

Substituting this into the differential equation, we find

$$
\begin{align}
 & \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n(n-1)c_{n}x^{n-2} - x\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^{n} = 0 \\ \\
 & \underbrace{ \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n(n-1)c_{n}x^{n-2} }_{ k = n-2 } - \underbrace{ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^{n+1} }_{ k= n+1 } = 0
\end{align}
$$

By shifting the index for each power-series, we can bring the two terms together under one summation. We do this by letting $k = n-2$ for the left term and $k = n+1$ for the right term. 

> The choice of $k$ in relation to the index $n_{i}$ has to do with making the exponents match. By making exponents match, we can group coefficients and find interesting patterns as you will see.

* For the left term, letting $k = n-2 \implies n = k+2$ and since the summation starts at $n = 2$, then the lower bound must be $k = 2 -2 = 0$.

* For the right term, letting $k = n+1 \to n = k - 1$ gives us that the summation should start at $k = 0+1 = 1$ since the summation starts at $n = 0$.

Hence, we can rewrite the expression as

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (k+2)(k+1)c_{k+2}x^{k} - \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} c_{k-1}x^{k} = 0
$$

And then by pulling the term where $k=0$ out of the left summation, we shift the index by one, making it possible now to group the power-series together under one summation.

$$
2c_{2} + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[ (k+1)(k+2)c_{k+2} - c_{k-1} \right] x^{k} = 0
$$

Now remember that the RHS of this equation is zero. We could say that the equation is true whenever $x = 0$ but our goal is for this equation to remain true for all $x$ in the interval. Hence by the [[Identity Property of Zero]], we could say that the coefficients of the power-series above are all zero, that is,

$$
\begin{align}
c_{2} = 0 \\ \\
(k+1)(k+2)c_{k+2} - c_{k-1} = 0
\end{align}
$$

> For clarification, we are not saying that every $c_{n}=0$ for $y = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^{n}$ but rather the coefficients for this differential equation must be zero. 

Further rewriting one of the above relations gives us a [[Recurrence Relation|recurrence relation]] of

$$
c_{k+2} = \frac{c_{k-1}}{(k+1)(k+2)}, \quad k = 1, 2, 3, ...
$$

> [!note]
> A general rule of thumb in writing recurrence relations is that we should express the coefficients of higher subscripts in terms of lower subscripts. In this case, express $c_{k+2}$ in terms of $c_{k-1}$. 
> 
> It's also important that for this to be a recurrence relation, we must include what $k$ is; otherwise, there's confusion as to where $k$ starts. That's informed by the lower summation bound of our power-series.

By taking successive values of $k$, we observe the following

$$
\begin{align}
 & k=1: & c_{3} = \frac{c_{0}}{2 \cdot 3} \\ \\
 & k=2: & c_{4} = \frac{c_{1}}{3 \cdot 4} \\ \\
 & k=3: & c_{5} = \frac{c_{2}}{4 \cdot 5} = 0 \\ \\
 & k=4: & c_{6} = \frac{c_{3}}{5 \cdot 6} = \frac{c_{0}}{2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 6} \\ \\
 & k=5: & c_{7} = \frac{c_{4}}{6 \cdot 7} = \frac{c_{1}}{3 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 \cdot 7} \\ \\
 & k=6: & c_{8} = \frac{c_{5}}{7 \cdot 8} = 0 \\ \\
 & k=7: & c_{9} = \frac{c_{6}}{8 \cdot 9} = \frac{c_{0}}{2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 6 \cdot 8 \cdot 9} \\ \\
 & k=8: & c_{10} = \frac{c_{7}}{9 \cdot 10} = \frac{c_{1}}{3 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 \cdot 7 \cdot 9 \cdot 10}
\end{align}
$$

> [!tip]
> It helps to leave the products still factored as it's easier to find closed form solutions of $c_{n}$ when possible


So if our proposed solution was $y = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^{n}$, we've essentially found the coefficients that solve the differential equation. We can use the proposed solution as our "template" and then substitute the values we've found for $c_{n}$.

$$
\begin{align}
y(x) &  = c_{0} + c_{1}x + c_{2}x^{2} + c_{3}x^{3} + c_{4}x^{4} + c_{5}x^{5} + c_{6}x^{6} + c_{7}x^{7} + \cdots \\ \\
 & = c_{0} + c_{1}x + \frac{c_{0}}{2 \cdot 3}x^{3} + \frac{c_{1}}{3 \cdot 4}x^{4}  + \frac{c_{0}}{2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 6}x^{6} + \frac{c_{1}}{3 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 \cdot 7}x^{7} + \cdots  & \text{Substituting} \\ \\
 & = c_{0} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{2 \cdot 3}x^{3} + \frac{1}{2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 6}x^{6} + \frac{1}{2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 6 \cdot 8 \cdot 9}x^{9} + \cdots \right) \\ \\ & \quad + c_{1} \left( x + \frac{1}{3 \cdot 4}x^{4} + \frac{1}{3 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 \cdot 7}x^{7} + \frac{1}{3 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 \cdot 7 \cdot 9 \cdot 10}x^{10} + \cdots \right)  & \text{Grouping terms}\\ \\
 & = c_{0} \left(  1 + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2 \cdot 3 \cdots (3k)(3k-1)}x^{3k} \right) + c_{1} \left( x + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{3 \cdot 4 \cdots (3k)(3k+1)}x^{3k+1} \right) & \text{Identify power-series} \\ \\
 & = c_{0}y_{1}(x) + c_{1}y_{2}(x) & \text{Form general solution}
\end{align}
$$

**Problem:** Solve the following differential equation 

$$
y'' - (1+x)y = 0
$$

**Approach:** Using previous definitions of $y$ and $y''$ into our DE

$$
\begin{align}
 & \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n(n-1)c_{n}x^{n-2} - (1+x) \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_{n}x^{n} = 0 \\ \\
 & \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n(n-1)c_{n}x^{n-2} - \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_{n}x^{n} - x \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_{n}x^{n} = 0 \\ \\
 & \underbrace{ \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n(n-1)c_{n}x^{n-2} }_{ k = n-2 } - \underbrace{ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_{n}x^{n} }_{ k = n } - \underbrace{ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_{n}x^{n+1} }_{ k = n+1 } = 0 \\ \\
 & \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (k+2)(k+1)c_{k+2}x^{k} - \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}x^{k} - \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k-1}x^{k} = 0 & \text{Shift the index} \\ \\
 & 2c_{2} - c_{0} + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left[ (k+1)(k+2)c_{k+2} - c_{k} - c_{k-1} \right]x^{k} = 0 & \text{Reshift index}
\end{align}
$$

By Identity Property, for $k = 1, 2, 3, ...$

$$
\begin{align}
 & 2c_{2} - c_{0} = 0 \implies c_{0} = 2c_{2} \\ \\
 & (k+1)(k+2)c_{k+2} - c_{k} - c_{k-1} = 0 \implies c_{k+2} = \frac{c_{k} + c_{k-1}}{(k+1)(k+2)}
\end{align}
$$

We know that the successive values of $c_{k+2}$ will be in terms of $c_{0}$ and $c_{1}$.  "To make life simple", as the textbook author writes, we can zero out $c_{0}$ or $c_{1}$ which allows us to find the coefficients in terms of only the other—easier for grouping into distinct power-series solutions. 

From our recurrence relation,

$$
\begin{array}{cc|c}
\text{Let} & c_{0} \ne 0, c_{1} = 0 & c_{0} = 0, c_{1} \ne 0 \\ \\
 & c_{2} = \frac{1}{2}c_{0} & c_{2} = \frac{1}{2}c_{0} = 0 \\ \\
 k=1 & c_{3} = \frac{c_{0}}{6} & c_{3} = \frac{c_{1}}{6} \\ \\
 k=2 & c_{4} = \frac{c_{2}}{3 \cdot 4} = \frac{c_{0}}{24} & c_{4} = \frac{c_{1}}{12} \\ \\
 k=3 & c_{5} = \frac{c_{3} + c_{2}}{4 \cdot 5} = \frac{c_{0}}{30} & c_{5} = \frac{c_{3}}{4 \cdot 5} = \frac{c_{1}}{120}
\end{array}
$$

Using those coefficients, we can form the general solution

$$
y(x) = c_{0} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{2}x^{2} + \frac{1}{6}x^{3} + \frac{1}{24}x^{4} + \frac{1}{30}x^{5} + \cdots \right) + c_{1} \left( x + \frac{1}{6}x^{3} + \frac{1}{12}x^{4} + \frac{1}{120}x^{5} + \cdots \right)  
$$
