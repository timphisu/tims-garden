
# Translations

## Translations in $s$

> [!theorem] First Translation Theorem
> If $\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} = F(s)$ and $a$ is any real number then $\mathcal{L}\{e^{ at }f(t)\} = F(s-a)$.

> **Proof:** It can be shown via integration that $(s-a)$ is the acting constant for $s$. 

$$
    \mathcal{L}\{e^{ at }f(t)\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }e^{ at }f(t) \, dt = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -(s-a)t }f(t)\, dt = F(s-a) 
$$

Notationally, it can be helpful to write

$$
\mathcal{L}\{e^{ at }f(t)\} = \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}_{s \to s-a}
$$

which means when we are doing the transform, we are replacing any instance of $s$ for $s-a$ in the transform.

From this perspective, we can actually justify one of our known formulas by recognizing $\mathcal{L}\{e^{ at }\}$ as $\mathcal{L}\{e^{ at }f(t)\}$ where $f(t) = 1$.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{e^{ at }\} = \mathcal{L}\{e^{ at } \cdot 1\} = \mathcal{L}\{1\}_{s \to s-a} = \frac{1}{s} \Big|_{s \to s-a} = \frac{1}{s-a}
$$

Inversely, the rule applies as

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s-a)\} = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s)\}_{s \to s-a} = e^{ at }f(t)
$$

> [!thought]
> Any shift in $s$ corresponds to an exponential function in $t$, and conversely, any exponential in $t$ transforms to a shift in $s$. 

---

**Problem:** Evaluate the following transforms

$$
\begin{align}
 & \mathcal{L}\{t^{10}e^{ -7t }\} \\ \\
 & \mathcal{L}\{e^{ -2t }\cos 4t\}
\end{align}
$$

**Approach:** For each we must recognize the exponential corresponds to a shift in $s$, while identifying and transforming the respective $f(t)$.

*For first problem*

$$
\mathcal{L}\{t^{10}e^{ -7t }\} = \mathcal{L}\{t^{10}\}_{s \to s+7} = \frac{10!}{s^{10+1}} \Big|_{s \to s+7}^{} = \frac{10!}{(s+7)^{11}}
$$

*For second problem*

$$
\mathcal{L}\{e^{ -2t } \cos 4t\} = \mathcal{L}\{\cos 4t\}_{s \to s+2} = \frac{s}{s^{2} + 4^{2}} \Big|_{s \to s+2}^{} = \frac{s+2}{(s+2)^{2} + 16}
$$

---

**Problem:** Evaluate the following 

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{s^{2} + 2s + 5} \right\}
$$

**Approach:** Since there are no real roots in the denominator, we cannot break this into a product of linear factors and do partial fraction decomposition. The other option is to recognize this as a $\sin kt / \cos kt$ form which begins with completing the square.

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{s^{2} + 2s + 5} \right\} = \underbrace{ \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{(s + 1)^{2} + 4} \right\} }_{ \text{Shift by -1} }U = \underbrace{ \frac{1}{2}e^{ -t }\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{2}{s^{2} + 2^{2}} \right\} }_{ k = 2 } = \frac{1}{2}e^{ -t } \sin 2t 
$$

> Notice how once I noticed a shift by -1, I pulled out a corresponding $e^{ at }$ in the next step where $a = -1$. 

> [!tip]
> For inverse transforms, using the bar notation the way we did for transforms is visually complicated and leaves room for error. I enjoy immediately pulling out a corresponding $e^{ at }$ from the inverse while replacing $s$ to $s-a$ so I can just focus on the form of $F(s)$. 

---

**Problem:** Evaluate the following inverse transform

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{(s+1)^{2}}{(s+2)^{4}} \right\}
$$

**Approach:** By inspection, the denominator is a transform of the $t^{n}$ form where there is a shift of $a = -2$. Expanding the numerator, we get

$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{s^{2} + 2s + 1}{(s+2)^{4}} \right\} &  = \underbrace{ \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{s^{2} + 2s}{(s+2)^{4}} \right\}  }_{ s(s+2) }+ \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{(s+2)^{4}} \right\} \\ \\
 & = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{s}{(s+2)^{3}} \right\} + \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{(s+2)^{4}} \right\}
\end{align}
$$

We can do partial fraction decomposition on the first split term:

$$
\begin{align}
 & \frac{s}{(s+2)^{3}} = \frac{A}{s+2} + \frac{B}{(s+2)^{2}} + \frac{C}{(s+2)^{3}} \\ \\
 & A(s+2)^{2} + B(s+2) + C = s
\end{align}
$$

By inspection, we recognize there is no $s^{2}$ term present on the RHS, which corresponds to $A = 0$. So, 

$$
\begin{align}
 & \cancel{ A(s+2)^{2} } + B(s+2) + C = s \\ \\
 & Bs + (2B + C) = s \\ \\
 & \implies B =1, C = -2
\end{align}
$$

Then,

$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{s^{2} + 2s + 1}{(s+2)^{4}} \right\} &  = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{  \frac{1}{(s+2)^{2}} - \frac{2}{(s+2)^{3}} + \frac{1}{(s+2)^{4}} \right\}
\end{align}
$$

We see that each denominator has a shift by $a = -2$, so by the previous theorem we can pull out a common exponential $e^{ at } = e^{ -2t }$ and then reshift the transform to $F(s)$ form. 

$$
\begin{align}
 & = e^{ -2t } \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{1}{s^{2}} - \frac{2}{s^{3}} + \frac{1}{s^{4}} \right\} \\ \\
 & = e^{ -2t } \left( t - t^{2} + \frac{1}{3!}t^{3} \right) 
\end{align}
$$

## Translations in $t$

> [!excerpt]
> The First Translation Theorem showed how to translate on the $s$-axis. A translation on the $t$-axis is essentially an adjust for moving the lower limit in the definition of a Laplace transform. This is accomplished via the **Heaviside function** or **unit step function**. 

### Introduction to the Unit Step Function

> [!definition]
> The **unit step function** is defined as 
> $$\mathcal{U}(t-a) = \begin{cases}0, & 0 \leq t < a \\ \\1, & t \geq a\end{cases}$$

whose graph looks like the following 

![[Pasted image 20260522105028.png|600]]


In this case, $a = 1$ and can be interpreted as being "off" when $0\leq t < a$ but then "on" for $t \geq a$. 

If we wanted the function to be first "on" and then "off", we can have $1 - \mathcal{U}(t - a)$ which gives

$$
1 - \mathcal{U}(t-a) = \begin{cases}
1 - 0, &  0\leq t<a \\ \\
1-1, & t \geq a
\end{cases} = \begin{cases}
1, & 0 \leq t < a \\ \\
0, & t\geq a
\end{cases}
$$

We can use the Heaviside function to "turn off and then on" certain functions at some point $a$ like $\sin t$ we could write

$$
\sin(t)\mathcal{U}(t-a) = \begin{cases}
0, & 0 \leq t < a \\ \\
\sin t, & t\geq a
\end{cases}
$$

whose graph looks like the following when $a = 1$

![[Pasted image 20260522110455.png|600]]

but consider $\sin(t-a)\mathcal{U}(t-a)$ whose graph would not only be shifted by $a$ units but also be "on" when $t\geq a$ but "off" or identically zero everywhere else when $0 \leq t < a$. 

![[Pasted image 20260522110751.png|600]]

### Second Translation Theorem

> [!theorem] Second Translation Theorem
> If $F(s) = \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}$ and $a > 0$ then $$ \mathcal{L}\{f(t-a)\mathcal{U}(t-a)\} = e^{ -as }F(s) $$

> [!warning]
> It's easy to interpret this theorem as $\mathcal{L}\{f(t-a)\mathcal{U}(t-a)\} = e^{ -as }\mathcal{L}\{f(t-a)\}$, but this is not true. The nuance to this theorem is that the RHS shows $F(s)$ not $F(s-a)$; do not bring along the shift of $a$ to your transform once you've pulled out your exponential. 

> **Proof:** Using the integral definition of the Laplace transform and then splitting it into two integrals since we have different function definitions based on the interval. 

$$
\begin{align}
 \mathcal{L}\{f(t-a)\mathcal{U}(t-a)\} &  = \int_{0}^{a} e^{ -st }f(t-a)\underbrace{ \mathcal{U}(t-a) }_{ \text{Zero for}\ 0 \leq t < a }  \, dt + \int_{a}^{\infty} e^{ -st }f(t-a)\underbrace{ \mathcal{U}(t-a) }_{ \text{1 for}\ t\geq a  }  \, dt  \\ \\
  & = \cancel{ \int_{0}^{a} e^{ -st }f(t-a)\mathcal{U}(t-a) \, dt } + \int_{a}^{\infty} e^{ -st }f(t-a) \, dt \\  
\end{align}
$$

If we do a substitution where $v  = (t-a)$, then $dv = dt$ and $t = v+a$. Since this is a definite integral, we must reshift our bounds where $v(a) = 0$ and $v(\infty) = \infty$. 

Our integral now becomes

$$
\begin{align}
 & = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -s(v+a) }f(v) \, dv = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -as }e^{ -sv }f(v) \, dv \\ \\
 & = e^{ -as } \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -sv }f(v) \, dv  
\end{align}
$$

The above integral is still the Laplace transform; only that our independent variable here is $v$ but gets transformed to a function in terms of $s$. 

And hence,

$$
= e^{ -as }F(s)
$$

> [!thought]
> In other words, if we shift our function's origin point from zero to $a$—turning it "on" at $a$, then that corresponds to an exponential $e^{ -as }$ in its Laplace transform. This is analogous to the First Translation Theorem but the other way around where, loosely speaking, a shift in $t$ corresponds to an exponential in $s$. 

Inversely, we find

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{e^{ -as }F(s)\} = f(t-a)\mathcal{U}(t-a)
$$

> This means we must replace $t$ for $(t-a)$ when inverse transforming $F(s)$. 

#### Alternative Form of the Theorem

Sometimes we encounter transforming a function like $g(t)\mathcal{U}(t-a)$ where the key difference is that the arguments don't match the way $f(t-a)\mathcal{U}(t-a)$ does. We compensate for this by shifting $g(t)$ to the left/opposite by $a$ units 

> Here we only consider $a > 0$ but this should work the other way around.

Recall the original theorem,

$$
\mathcal{L}\{f(t-a)\mathcal{U}(t-a)\} = e^{ -as }\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}
$$

If we let $g(t) = f(t-a)$, which is a shift of $f(t)$'s graph by $a$ units to the right, then from the perspective of $g(t)$, the graph of $f(t)$ is shifted to the left. That is, $g(t) = f(t-a) \implies f(t) = g(t+a)$.

We can explain this relation graphically:

![[Pasted image 20260525105838.png|600]]

Hence,

$$
\boxed{ \mathcal{L}\{g(t)\mathcal{U}(t-a)\} = e^{ -as }\mathcal{L}\{g(t+a)\} } 
$$

> From the original form of the Second Translation Theorem, if arguments match, the transform does not need to bring any shift. But when arguments don't match, the transform does bring along a shift.
