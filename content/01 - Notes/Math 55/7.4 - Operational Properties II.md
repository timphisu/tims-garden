
# Derivatives of Laplace Transforms

It can be shown that

> [!theorem]
> If $\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} = F(s)$ and $n = 1, 2, 3, ...$ then $$ \mathcal{L}\{t^{n}f(t)\} = - (1)^{n} \frac{d^{n}}{ds^{n}} F(s) $$

For $n = 1$ and $n=2$, we get the following results from the above theorem:

$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}\{tf(t)\} &  = -\frac{d}{ds} \left[ F(s) \right] \\  \\
\mathcal{L}\{t^{2}f(t)\}  & = \frac{d^{2} }{ds^{2}} \left[ F(s) \right] 
\end{align}
$$

> which we can use for common problems

### Intuition

The intuition for this theorem comes from the integral definition of the Laplace transform. 

Consider taking the first derivative with respect to $s$ of the Laplace transform:

$$
\frac{d}{ds} \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{ \partial  }{ \partial s } e^{ -st }f(t) \, dt = - \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }tf(t) \, dt = -\mathcal{L}\{tf(t)\}  
$$

Taking second-derivatives and so on would prove the above theorem.

## Recognizing Derivatives to Solve IVPs

If we recognize $G(s) = \frac{d}{ds}F(s)$ as the derivative of another function $F(s)$ which we know the inverse for, that is, $\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s)\} = f(t)$ then the previous theorem inversely applies as 

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{d}{ds}F(s) \right\} = -t \cdot \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s)\}
$$

Let $a$ be a scalar where $a < 0$ then by the linearity of inverse transform and derivative operator:

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{d}{ds} \left[ aF(s) \right]  \right\} = at\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s)\}
$$

---

**Problem:** Use the Laplace transform to solve the given initial-value problem.

$$
y'' + 36y = f(t), \quad y(0) = 0, \quad y'(0) = 1 \quad \text{where} \quad f(t) = \begin{cases}
\cos(6t), & 0 \leq t < \pi \\
0, & t \geq \pi
\end{cases}
$$

**Approach:** Rewriting $f(t)$ in terms of unit step functions we get 

$$
\begin{align}
f(t) &  = \cos(6t) \left[ 1 - \mathcal{U}(t-\pi) \right] \\ \\
 &  = \cos 6t - \cos (6t) \mathcal{U}(t-\pi)
\end{align}
$$

> We do this process so that $f(t)$ is actually able to be transformed

Taking the Laplace transform on both sides of the equation, it's better to deal with the RHS since it's a bit much to do all in one go.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} = \mathcal{L}\{\cos 6t\} + e^{ -\pi s } \mathcal{L}\{\cos 6(t+\pi)\}
$$

by the Alternative Form of the Second Translation Theorem. If we focus our attention on the second term, we can use the Sum Law of Cosine to find

$$
\begin{align}
\cos (6t + 6\pi) &  = \cos(6t)\cos(6\pi) - \sin(6t)\sin(6\pi) \\ \\
 & = \cos(6t) \cdot 1 - \sin(6t) \cdot 0 & \sin(k\pi) = 0, \cos(2k\pi) = 1 \\ \\
 & = \cos(6t)
\end{align}
$$

Hence the Laplace transform is really 

$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} & = \mathcal{L}\{\cos 6t \} + e^{ -\pi s } \mathcal{L}\{\cos 6t\} \\ \\
 & = \frac{s}{s^{2} + 36} + e^{ -\pi s } \left( \frac{s}{s^{2} + 36} \right) 
\end{align}
$$

The transformed DE is 

$$
\begin{align}
 & \mathcal{L}\{y''\} + 36 \mathcal{L}\{y\} = \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} \\ \\
 & s^{2}Y(s) - sy(0) - y'(0) + 36Y(s) = \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} \\ \\
 & s^{2}Y(s) - 1 + 36Y(s) = \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} \\ \\
 & (s^{2} + 36)Y(s) = \frac{s}{s^{2} + 36} + e^{ -\pi s } \left( \frac{s}{s^{2} + 36} \right)  + 1 \\ \\
\end{align}
$$

Solving for $Y(s)$ by dividing by $s^{2} + 36$, 

$$
Y(s) = \frac{s}{(s^{2} + 36)^{2}} - e^{ -\pi s } \left[ \frac{s}{(s^{2} + 36)^{2}} \right] + \frac{1}{s^{2} + 36}
$$

Consider the expression $\displaystyle \frac{s}{(s^{2} + 36)^{2}}$, its antiderivative can be found as

$$
\int \frac{s}{(s^{2} + 36)^{2}}\, ds = \frac{1}{2} \int \frac{1}{(s^{2} + 36)^{2}}\, d(s^{2} + 36) = -\frac{1}{2} \int d \left( \frac{1}{s^{2} + 36} \right) 
$$

Since $\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds} \left[ -\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{s^{2} + 36} \right] = \frac{s}{(s^{2} + 36)^{2}}$, then by the theorem inversely applied 

$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{s}{(s^{2} + 36)^{2}} \right\} &  =  \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{  \frac{d}{ds} \left[  -\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{s^{2} + 36}  \right]   \right\} \\ \\
 &  = -\frac{1}{2} \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{  \frac{d}{ds} \left[ \frac{1}{s^{2} + 36} \right]   \right\} & \text{Linearity of Transforms}\\ \\
  &  = \frac{1}{2}t\mathcal{L}^{-1}\underbrace{ \left\{ \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{6}{s^{2} + 36} \right\} }_{ k = 6 } & \text{Multiply by}\ -t \\ \\
 & = \frac{1}{12}t \sin 6t
\end{align}
$$

So applying the inverse to $Y(s)$, we get

$$
\begin{align}
y(t) &  = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{s}{(s^{2} + 36)^{2}} \right\} + \mathcal{L}^{-1}\underbrace{ \left\{ e^{ -\pi s } \left[ \frac{s}{(s^{2} + 36)^{2}} \right]   \right\} }_{ \text{Shift in}\ t\ \text{by}\ \pi } + \mathcal{L}^{-1}\underbrace{ \left\{ \frac{1}{s^{2} + 36} \right\} }_{ k = 6 }\\ \\
 & = \frac{1}{12}t\sin 6t + \mathcal{U}(t- \pi) \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{s}{s^{2} + 36} \right\}_{t \to t-\pi} + \frac{1}{6}\sin(6t)\\ \\
 & = \frac{1}{12}t\sin 6t -\frac{1}{12} \mathcal{U}(t- \pi)\left[ (t-\pi)\sin 6(t-\pi) \right]  + \frac{1}{6}\sin(6t)
\end{align}
$$


# Integrals of Transforms

On top of the Laplace transform whose resulting function was only in terms of $s$, we introduce a new transform whose resulting function is still in terms of $t$. 

> [!definition]
> Let $f$ and $g$ be functions of $t$. Then the **convolution** of $f$ and $g$, denoted ${f}\ * \ {g}$, is defined as $$ {f}\ * \ {g} = \int_{0}^{t} f(\tau)g(t- \tau) \, d\tau  $$ 

> Taking the convolution uses previously known techniques to taking the Laplace transform by integral definition. There's no value in me showing you how to take the convolution. 

A property of the convolution of $f$ and $g$ is that order does not matter, that is, ${f}\ * \ {g} = {g}\ * \ {f}$.

It can be shown that

> [!theorem]
> If $f(t)$ and $g(t)$ are piecewise continuous on $[0, \infty)$ and of exponential order then $$ \mathcal{L}\{{f}\ * \ {g}\} = \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}\mathcal{L}\{g(t)\} = F(s)G(s) $$

Inversely, we can recognize a transform as the product of two other transforms which would lead to

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s)G(s)\} = \int_{0}^{t} f(\tau)g(t-\tau) \, d\tau \quad \text{or} \quad \int_{0}^{t} g(\tau)f(t-\tau) \, d\tau  
$$

and after evaluating the convolution, we get the inverse transform. 

---

**Problem:** Evaluate the given Laplace transform without evaluating the convolution integral.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{{e^{ -t }}\ * \ {e^{ t }\cos t}\}
$$

**Approach:** We know by the Convolution Theorem for Laplace transforms that

$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}\{{e^{ -t }}\ * \ {e^{ t }\cos t}\} &  = \mathcal{L}\{e^{ -t }\} \cdot \mathcal{L}\underbrace{ \{e^{ t }\cos t\} }_{ \text{Shift in}\ s\ \text{by}\ 1} \\ \\
 & = \frac{1}{s+1} \cdot \mathcal{L}\{\cos t\}_{s \to s-1} \\ \\
 & = \frac{1}{s+1} \cdot \frac{s-1}{(s-1)^{2} + 1}
\end{align}
$$

---

**Problem:** Evaluate the given Laplace transform.

$$
\mathcal{L}\left\{ \int_{0}^{t}  \tau e^{ t-\tau } \, d\tau  \right\}
$$

**Approach:** We recognize the function being transformed is a convolution. It's just a matter of identifying what $f$ and $g$ are. In the order of our notation, we see that $f(t) = t$ and $g(t) = e^{ t }$, which then means

$$
\mathcal{L}\left\{ \int_{0}^{t} \tau e^{ t-\tau } \, d\tau  \right\} = \mathcal{L}\{t\} \cdot \mathcal{L}\{e^{ t }\} = \frac{1}{s^{2}} \cdot \frac{1}{s-1}
$$

---

**Problem:** Evaluate given Laplace transform. 

$$
\mathcal{L}\left\{ t \int_{0}^{t} \sin \tau \, d\tau  \right\}
$$

**Approach:** Since there's a factor of $t$ present in our transform, we know by the Derivative Theorem that

$$
\mathcal{L}\left\{ t \int_{0}^{t} \sin \tau \, d\tau  \right\} = - \frac{d}{ds} \mathcal{L}\left\{ \int_{0}^{t} \sin \tau \, d\tau  \right\}
$$

Looking at our convolution, it seems there's only one function $f(t) = \sin(t)$ present. However, if we recognize that $g(t-\tau) = 1$ is being multiplied onto $f(t)$, then any shift in a constant function would still be $g(t) = 1$. 

So,

$$
\begin{align}
 \mathcal{L}\left\{ t \int_{0}^{t} \sin \tau \, d\tau  \right\} &  = - \frac{d}{ds} \mathcal{L}\left\{ \int_{0}^{t} \sin \tau \, d\tau  \right\} \\ \\
  & = -\frac{d}{ds} \left[ \mathcal{L}\{\sin(t)\}\cdot\mathcal{L}\{1\} \right] \\ \\
  & = -\frac{d}{ds} \left[ \frac{1}{s^{2} + 1} \cdot \frac{1}{s} \right] \\ \\
  & = -\frac{d}{ds} \left[ \frac{1}{s^{3} + s} \right] = -\frac{d}{ds} (s^{3} + s)^{-1} \\ \\
  & = \frac{3s^{2} + 1}{(s^{3} + s)^{2}}
\end{align}
$$


# Periodic Functions 

Functions like $\sin(t)$ and $\cos(t)$ are periodic with an interval $T = 2\pi$.  If $T$ is the period, a function $f$ is said to be periodic if it's piecewise defined on the interval $[0, T]$ and $f(t + T) = f(t)$.

An example of this may be 

$$
f(t) = \begin{cases}
t, & 0 \leq t < 1 \\ \\
2-t, & 1 \leq t < 2
\end{cases} \qquad \text{where} \quad f(t+2) = f(t)
$$

What would the Laplace transform look like for this function?

> [!theorem]
> If $f(t)$ is piecewise continuous on $[0, \infty)$, of exponential order, and periodic with period $T$, then $$ \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} = \frac{1}{1-e^{ -sT }} \int_{0}^{T}e^{ -st }f(t)\, dt $$
