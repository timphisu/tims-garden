## Introduction to Transforms

> [!definition]
> **Transform** refers to the result of doing a process to a function, thereby transforming to another function (e.g. differentiation and integration)

Transforms, may also be called an **operator**, possess linearity properties. For example, differentiation is an operator and can do the following

$$
\frac{d}{dx} \left[ af(x) + bg(x) \right] = a \frac{d}{dx} f(x) + b \frac{d}{dx} g(x)
$$

# Laplace Transform

> [!definition]
> Let $f(t)$ be a function defined for $t \geq 0$. Then the integral $$\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }f(t)\, dt$$ is said to be the **Laplace transform** of $f$, where $K(s, t) = e^{ -st }$ is called the *kernel*, provided that the integral converges. 

Notationally, we write $\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} = F(s)$ which refers to the resulting function in terms of $s$, which as of my knowledge (5/13) $s$ is somewhat of a dummy variable. 
## Examples of Using the Integral Definition

We can immediately use the definition to find some basic results. Much of the results are driven by using integration by parts. 

**Problem:** Evaluate $\mathcal{L}\{1\}$, that is find $\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}$ where $f(t) = 1$.

**Approach:** Using the integral process

$$
\mathcal{L}\{1\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }\, dt = - \frac{1}{s} \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }d(-st) =  - \frac{1}{s} \Big[ e^{ -st } \Big]_{t=0}^{\lim_{ t \to \infty } }
$$

When dealing with this improper integral, we know what the value converges to. Since $\lim_{ t \to \infty }e^{ -st } = 0$ and $e^{ -st }|_{t = 0} = 1$

$$
- \frac{1}{s} \left( \lim_{ t \to \infty } e^{ -st } - e^{0} \right)  = -\frac{1}{s} (0 - 1) = \frac{1}{s}  
$$

Hence, 

$$
\mathcal{L}\{1\} = \frac{1}{s}, \quad s > 0
$$

> Even though the value of $s$ is arbitrary, we still write $s>0$ as a formality.

---

**Problem:** Evaluate $\mathcal{L}\{t\}$

**Approach:** Using the integral definition of the Laplace transform

$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}\{t\} &  = \int_{0}^{\infty} te^{ -st }\, dt = -\frac{1}{s} \int_{0}^{\infty} t\, d\left( e^{ -st } \right) \\ \\
 & = -\frac{1}{s} \left( te^{ -st }\Big|_{t = 0}^{t = \infty } - \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }\, dt \right) 
\end{align}
$$

For $\displaystyle \frac{t}{e^{ st }}$, the denominator $e^{ st }$ grows faster than the numerator, hence $\lim_{ t \to \infty }te^{ -st } = 0$.

$$
\begin{align}
 & = -\frac{1}{s} \left(\ \cancel{ \lim_{ t \to \infty } te^{ -st } - te^{ -st }\Big|_{t = 0} } - \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }\, dt \ \right) \\ \\
 & = \frac{1}{s} \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }\, dt \\ \\
 & = -\frac{1}{s^{2}} \Big[ e^{ -st } \Big]_{t = 0}^{t=\infty} \\ \\
 & = \frac{1}{s^{2}}
\end{align}
$$

Hence,

$$
\mathcal{L}\{t\} = \frac{1}{s^{2}}, \quad s>0
$$

---

**Problem:** Evaluate $\mathcal{L}\{\sin(kt)\}$ where $k$ is a scalar.

**Approach:** We use two cycles of integration by parts until the term terminates upon itself. 

$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}\{\sin (kt)\}  & = \underbrace{ \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }\sin(kt) \, dt }_{ I } = -\frac{1}{s} \int_{0}^{\infty} \sin(kt)\, d\left( e^{ -st } \right) \\ \\
 & = -\frac{1}{s} \left( e^{ -st }\sin(kt) \Big|_{t = 0}^{t=\infty} - \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }\, d\sin(kt) \right) \\ \\
 & = -\frac{1}{s} \left( \ \cancel{ \lim_{ t \to \infty } e^{ -st }\sin(kt) - e^{ -st }\sin(kt)|_{t = 0} } - k \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }\cos (kt)\, dt \right) \\ \\
 & = \frac{k}{s} \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }\cos(kt)\, dt = -\frac{k}{s^{2}} \int_{0}^{\infty} \cos(kt)\,  d(e^{ -st }) \\ \\
 & = -\frac{k}{s^{2}} \left( e^{ -st }\cos(kt) \Big|_{t = 0}^{t = \infty} - \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }\, d\cos(kt) \right) \\ \\
 & =  -\frac{k}{s^{2}} \left( \ \cancel{ \lim_{ t \to \infty } \frac{\cos(kt)}{e^{ st }} } - e^{ -st }\cos(kt)|_{t = 0} + k \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }\sin(kt) \right) \\ \\
I &  = - \frac{k}{s^{2}} \left( -1 + kI \right) = \frac{k}{s^{2}} - \frac{k^{2}}{s^{2}}I \\ \\ \\
 \left( 1 + \frac{k^{2}}{s^{2}} \right)I & = \frac{k}{s^{2}} \implies \left( \frac{s^{2} + k^{2}}{s^{2}} \right)I = \frac{k}{s^{2}} \implies I = \frac{k}{s^{2} + k^{2}}
\end{align}
$$

Hence,

$$
\mathcal{L}\{\sin(kt)\} = \frac{k}{s^{2} + k^{2}}
$$

### Piecewise Solutions

Consider the following example

**Problem:** Use the definition of the Laplace transform to find $\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}$ where 

$$
f(t) = \begin{cases}
2t + 1, & 0 \leq t < 1 \\ \\
0, & t \geq 1
\end{cases}
$$
**Approach:** For this piecewise-defined function, we should split the integral into two definite integrals where we integrate upon the respective subintervals.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{ -st }f(t)\, dt = \int_{0}^{1} e^{ -st }f(t)\, dt + \int_{1}^{\infty}e^{ -st }f(t)\, dt
$$

Using the definitions of $f(t)$ at those subintervals, we get

$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} & = \int_{0}^{1} e^{ -st }(2t+1)\, dt + \cancel{ \int_{1}^{\infty} e^{ -st } \cdot 0\, dt } \\ \\
 & = -\frac{1}{s} \left( e^{ -st }(2t+1) \Big|_{t = 0}^{t = 1} - \int_{0}^{1} e^{ -st }\, d(2t+1) \right) \\ \\
 & = -\frac{1}{s} \left[ \left( 3e^{ -s }  - 1 \right) - 2\int_{0}^{1} e^{ -st }\, dt  \right] \\ \\
 & = -\frac{1}{s} \left[ 3e^{ -s } - 1 +\frac{2}{s} \left[ e^{ -st } \right]_{t = 0}^{t = 1} \right] \\ \\
 & = -\frac{1}{s} \left[ 3e^{ -s } - 1 + \frac{2}{s}(e^{ -s } - 1) \right] \\ \\
 & = -\frac{1}{s} \left( 3e^{ -s } - 1 + \frac{2}{s}e^{ -s }- \frac{2}{s} \right) \\ \\
 & = -\frac{3e^{ -s }}{s} + \frac{1}{s} - \frac{2e^{ -s }}{s^{2}} + \frac{2}{s^{2}} \\ \\
 & = \frac{1}{s}\left( 1 - 2e^{ -s } \right) + \frac{1}{s^{2}}\left( 2 - 3e^{ -s } \right)  
\end{align}
$$

### Demonstrating the Linearity Property

The *Laplace transform* is by name a transform and an operator, hence it holds <u>linearity properties</u>:

$$
\mathcal{L}\{\alpha f(t) + \beta g(t)\} = \alpha F(s) + \beta G(s)
$$

We can use the linearity property of the Laplace transform to breakdown functions that are sums of other functions including their scalar multiples.

**Problem:** Evaluate $\mathcal{L}\{4t-7\}$

**Approach:** 

$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}\{4t-7\} & = 4 \mathcal{L}\{t\} - 7 \mathcal{L}\{1\} & \text{Linearity of Laplace Transform} \\ \\
 & = \frac{4}{s^{2}} - \frac{7}{s} & \mathcal{L}\{t\} = \frac{1}{s^{2}}, \mathcal{L}\{1\} = \frac{1}{s}
\end{align}
$$
---

**Problem:** Evaluate $\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}$ where $f(t) = e^{ -2t-5 }$

**Approach:** If we recognize that $e^{ -2t-5 } = e^{ -2t }e^{ -5 }$ and hence we can pull a scalar multiple out.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{e^{ -2t-5 }\} = e^{ -5 } \mathcal{L}\{e^{ -2t }\}
$$

Recall the form of the Laplace transform for $e^{ t }$, that is $\displaystyle \mathcal{L}\{e^{ at }\} = \frac{1}{s-a}$ . Recognizing $a = -2$ in this case:

$$
\mathcal{L}\{e^{ -2t-5 }\} = \frac{e^{ -5 }}{s + 2}
$$


## Common Laplace Transforms


$$\begin{align} & (a) & \mathcal{L}\{1\} = \frac{1}{s} \\ \\ & (b) & \mathcal{L}\{t^{n}\} = \frac{n!}{s^{n+1}}\ \text{for}\ n = 1, 2, 3, ... \\ \\ & (c) & \mathcal{L}\{e^{ at }\} = \frac{1}{s-a} \\ \\ & (d) & \mathcal{L}\{\sin kt\} = \frac{k}{s^{2} + k^{2}} \\ \\ & (e) & \mathcal{L}\{\cos kt\} = \frac{s}{s^{2} + k^{2}} \\ \\ & (f) & \mathcal{L}\{\sinh kt\} = \frac{k}{s^{2} - k^{2}} \\ \\ & (g) & \mathcal{L}\{\cosh kt\} = \frac{s}{s^{2} - k^{2}} \end{align}$$


> It might also be helpful to know that $\mathcal{L}\{0)\} = 0$, for future reference in solving homogeneous DEs.
## Exponential Order

> [!definition]
> A function $f$ is said to be of **exponential order** if there exist constants $c$, $M>0$, and $T>0$ such that $|f(t)| < Me^{ ct }$ for all $t > T$

> In other words, a function $f$ is of *exponential order* when $e^{ x }$ grows faster than $f(x)$. That's why we are able to converge the improper integral because the limit $\lim_{ t \to \infty } e^{ -st }f(t) = 0$.

Examples of functions that are of exponential order include:

$$
t \leq e^{ t }, \quad \cos t \leq e^{ t }, \quad e^{ -t } \leq e^{ t }
$$

granted that we're considering the absolute values of these functions and on the interval $(0, \infty)$.

### Sufficient Conditions for Existence

> [!theorem]
> If $f$ is piecewise continuous on $[0, \infty)$ and of exponential order, then $\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}$ exists for $s > c$. 

In other words, a function being of exponential order guarantees a Laplace transform exists, provided it's on an interval greater than or equal to zero.

### Decay of Laplace Transform

> [!theorem]
> If $f$ is piecewise continuous on $[0, \infty)$ and of exponential order and $F(s) = \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}$, then $\lim_{ s \to \infty }F(s) = 0$.

As $s$ gets moves further away from zero, the Laplace transform converges to zero.
