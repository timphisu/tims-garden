# Logical Operators 

A **proposition** is a declarative statement (asserts an idea rather than a command or interrogative) that has a single truth value such as $T$ or $F$. Propositions can be denoted in variables $p,r, s, t$ . A proposition which cannot be broken down any further is called an *atomic proposition*.

We use **logic operators** or *connectives* to combine simple propositions to make *compound statements*
#### Negation

The proposition $\neg p$ is read "not $p$" or "It is not the case that $p$". 

Here's a **truth table** for the negation of $p$ which shows the possible truth values of $p$ and what negating $p$ does to the truth values

| $$p$$ | $$\neg p$$ |
| ----- | ---------- |
| T     | F          |
| F     | T          |

## Conjunction and Disjunction

The **conjunction** of $p$ and $q$ denoted $p \land q$ is read as "$p$ and $q$". The conjunction $p \land q$ is only true when both $p$ and $q$ are true. 

The **disjunction** (also called *inclusive "or"*), denoted $p \lor q$, is read as "$p$ or $q$". A disjunction is true when at least one of the two propositions are true. 

An example of this may be, "If you take algebra or trigonometry, you may take Calculus". Let's denote $p$ as taking algebra and $q$ as you taking trig, then the whole statement can be expressed $p \lor q$. If $p$ or $q$ is true, the whole "either or" statement to be true, which is *sufficient* enough for you to take calculus. 

We may also have the *exclusive "or"* of $p$ and $q$, also known as **XOR**, denoted as $p \oplus q$  where the truth value depends on if there's a difference of truth values between $p$ and $q$, that is, one is true and the other is false.

| $$p$$ | $$q$$ | $$p \land q$$ | $$p \lor q$$ | $$p \oplus q$$ |
| ----- | ----- | ------------- | ------------ | -------------- |
| T     | T     | T             | T            | F              |
| T     | F     | F             | T            | T              |
| F     | T     | F             | T            | T              |
| F     | F     | F             | F            | F              |


A conditional statement or the **implication** $p \to q$ is the proposition "if $p$, then $q$" . In a conditional statement, $p$ is called the *hypothesis* (or antecedent or premise) and $q$ is called the *conclusion* (or consequence).


| $$p$$ | $$q$$ | $$p \to q$$ |
| ----- | ----- | ----------- |
| T     | T     | T           |
| T     | F     | F           |
| F     | T     | T           |
| F     | F     | T           |

> [!thought]
> The expression "$p$ only if q" means that $q$ is required for $p$ and this is further reinforced with the idea that if "no $q$ then no $p$" ($\neg q \to \neg p$) which is the contrapositive. That is the same way as saying there's no other way $p$ happens (only <u>one</u> way) without $q$ happening—conditional.
#### Converse, Contrapositive, and Inverse

Based on the condition $p \to q$, we can create new conditions:

* The proposition $q \to p$ is called the **converse** where the flow of logic goes backwards. 

* The **contrapositive** $p \to q$ is the proposition $\neg q \to \neg p$  where the negation of the conclusion implies the reverse truth of the hypothesis.

	* What we find is that the contrapositive has always the same truth value as $p \to q$

* The **inverse** of $p \to q$ is the proposition $\neg p \to \neg q$ where the reverse truth of the hypothesis means the reverse truth of the conclusion.

When truth values are the same between compound propositions, regardless of the truths of the simpler proposition, they are considered **equivalent**

##### Examples of Variations of "If-Else" Statements

**Problem**: Find the contrapositive, the converse, and the inverse of the conditional statement.

<div style="text-align: center;">"The home team wins whenever it is raining"</div>

**Approach**: We can transform the statement "$q$ whenever $p$" to an equivalent statement "if $p$, then $q$" which is more workable 

"If it is raining, then the home team wins."

The contrapositive is 

> "If the home team does not win, then it is not raining."

The converse is

> "If the home team wins, then it is raining."

The inverse is 

> "If it is not raining, then the home team does not win."

### Biconditionals

A **biconditional statement** $p \leftrightarrow q$ is the proposition "$p$ if and only if $q$" where the statement is only true when $p$ and $q$ are both true; otherwise, it's false. This shows how the flow of logic goes both ways. 

| $$p$$ | $$q$$ | $$p \leftrightarrow q$$ |
| ----- | ----- | ----------------------- |
| T     | T     | T                       |
| T     | F     | F                       |
| F     | T     | F                       |
| F     | F     | T                       |

#### *Precedence of Logical Operators*

1. The negation $\neg$ is applied before all other operators

2. The logical "and" $\land$ comes before v

3. The logical "or" $\lor$ 

4. The implication operator $\to$ comes before

5. The biconditional operator $\leftrightarrow$

### [[Truth Tables|Truth Tables]]

We can use truth tables to determine the truth values of compound propositions, where each separate column shows the truth values for each expression

#### Example 1.1.14

##### Problem

Construct the truth table of the compound proposition

$$
(p \lor \neg q) \to (p \land q)
$$

##### Approach

The compound proposition uses the proposition variables $p, q$ so we begin our table by making pairs of $T, F$ . The left expression uses $\neg p$ so we make a new column with its truth values, then use that to build up to a new column $(p \lor \neg q)$ . Lastly, a column is added for $(p \land q)$ will be used for the final column $(p \lor \neg q) \to (p \land q)$ which is the proposition we were determining its values.
