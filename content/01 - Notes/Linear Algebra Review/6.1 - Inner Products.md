# Inner Products

> [!definition]
> An **inner product** on a real vector space $V$ is a function that associates a real number $\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle$ with each pair of vectors in $V$ in such a way that the following axioms are satisfied for vectors $\vec{u}, \vec{v}, \vec{w}$ in $V$ and all scalars $k$:
> 
> 1. Symmetry Axiom: $\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle = \langle \vec{v}, \vec{u} \rangle$
> 2. Additivity Axiom: $\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} + \vec{w} \rangle = \langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle + \langle \vec{u}, \vec{w} \rangle$
> 3. Homogeneity Axiom: $\left\langle {k\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle= k \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle$
> 4. Positivity Axiom: $\left\langle {\vec{v}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle \geq 0$ with the equality being true if and only if $\vec{v} = \vec{0}$. 
> 
> A real vector space with an inner product is called a *real inner product space*.

An inner product is based on the dot product and its properties. The "dot product" or the *Euclidean inner product* or *standard inner product* on $R^{n}$, which is defined as

$$
\left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle = u_{1}v_{1} + u_{2}v_{2} + \cdots + u_{n}v_{n}
$$

for vectors $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ in $R^{n}$, is known to satisfy the *inner product space axioms*.

> [!note]
> We call it the Euclidean inner product as to distinguish it from other inner products that can be defined with $R^{n}$. This speaks to the fact that a vector space does not need to have a unique inner product. You could define any inner product on a vector space so long as they satisfy the axioms. 

---

Here's an example of an expression that does not define an inner product on its vector space.

**Problem:** Determine whether the expression defines an inner product on $R^{3}$, and if not, list list all inner product axioms that fail to hold.

$$
\left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle = u_{1}^{2}v_{1}^{2} + u_{2}^{2}v_{2}^{2} + u_{3}^{2}v_{3}^{2}
$$

**Approach:** Let's test each axiom if it holds under this formula.

For the Axiom of Symmetry, the formula holds because of commutativity of multiplication. 

However for Additivity,

$$
\begin{align}
\left\langle {\vec{u} + \vec{v}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle  & = (u_{1} + v_{1})^{2}w_{1}^{2} + (u_{2} + v_{2})^{2} w_{2}^{2} + (u_{3} + v_{3})^{2} w_{3}^{2} \\ \\
 & = u_{1}^{2}w_{1}^{2} + u_{2}^{2}w_{2}^{2} + u_{3}^{2}w_{3}^{2} + v_{1}^{2}w_{1}^{2} + v_{2}^{2}w_{2}^{2} + v_{3}^{2}w_{3}^{2} \\
  & + 2u_{1}v_{1}w_{1}^{2} + 2u_{2}v_{2}w_{2}^{2} = 2u_{3}v_{3}w_{3}^{2} \\ \\
 & = \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle  + \left\langle {\vec{v}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle  + 2(u_{1}v_{1}w_{1}^{2} + u_{2}v_{2}w_{2}^{2} + u_{3}v_{3}w_{3}^{2}) \\ \\
 & \ne \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle + \left\langle {\vec{v}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle 
\end{align}
$$

So it fails

Homogeneity fails because the scaling would not be linear. 

Positivity holds because 

$$
\begin{align}
\| \vec{u} \|  & = \sqrt{ \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{u}} \right\rangle  } \\ \\
 & = \sqrt{ u_{1}^{2}u_{1}^{2} + u_{2}^{2}u_{2}^{2} + u_{3}^{2}u_{3}^{2} } \\ \\
 & = \sqrt{ u_{1}^{4} + u_{2}^{4} + u_{3}^{4} }
\end{align}
$$

which is greater than zero for any vector that has at least a single nonzero component. Then if $\| \vec{u} \| = 0$, that implies that every component must be zero, that is $\vec{u} = \vec{0}$ in $R^{ 3}$. The logic would hold conversely. 

## Norm and Distance

> [!definition]
> If $V$ is a real inner product space, then the **norm** (or *length*) of a vector $\vec{v}$ in $V$ is denoted by $\| \vec{v} \|$ and is defined by
> 
> $$ \| \vec{v} \| = \sqrt{ \left\langle {\vec{v}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle  } $$
> 
> and the **distance** between two vectors is denoted by $d(\vec{u}, \vec{v})$ and is defined by 
> 
> $$
> d(\vec{u}, \vec{v}) = \| \vec{u} - \vec{v} \| = \sqrt{ \left\langle {\vec{u} - \vec{v}}, {\vec{u} - \vec{v}} \right\rangle  }
> $$
> 
> A vector of norm $1$ is called a **unit vector**.

**Problem:** Let $R^{2}$ have the weighted Euclidean inner product

$$
\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle = 2u_{1}v_{1} + 3u_{2}v_{2}
$$

and let $\vec{u} = (1,1)$ and $\vec{v} = (3,2)$. Compute the norm of both vectors and the distance between them. 

**Approach:** Taking the norm squared of $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ just involves

$$
\begin{align}
\| \vec{u} \|^{2} &  = 2u_{1}^{2} + 3u_{2}^{2} = 2 \cdot 1^{2} + 3 \cdot_{1}^{2} = 2 + 3 = 5 \\ \\
\| \vec{v} \|  & = 2v_{1}^{2} + 3v_{2}^{2} = 2 \cdot 3 ^{2} + 3 \cdot 2^{2} = 18 + 12 = 30 
\end{align}
$$

So,

$$
\| \vec{u} \| = \sqrt{ 5 }, \| \vec{v} \|  = \sqrt{ 30 }
$$

We can use the inner product space axioms to our use since we already know the norms of $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$, so the distance between them can be expressed in terms of their norms and inner product. 

$$
\begin{align}
[d(\vec{u}, \vec{v})]^{2} &  = \| \vec{u} - \vec{v} \| ^{2} = \left\langle {\vec{u} - \vec{v}}, {\vec{u} - \vec{v}} \right\rangle \\ \\
 & = \left\langle {\vec{u} - \vec{v}}, {\vec{u}} \right\rangle - \left\langle {\vec{u} - \vec{v}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle  & \text{Additivity and Homogeniety}\\ \\
 & = \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{u}} \right\rangle  - \left\langle {\vec{v}}, {\vec{u}} \right\rangle  - \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle + \left\langle {\vec{v}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle \\ \\
 & =  \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{u}} \right\rangle  - 2\left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle + \left\langle {\vec{v}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle  & \text{Symmetry} \\ \\
 & = \| \vec{u} \| ^{2} - 2 \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle + \| \vec{v} \| ^{2} & \text{Definition of Norm} \\ \\
\end{align}
$$

All that's required is to find $\left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle$,

$$
\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle = 2u_{1}v_{1} + 3u_{2}v_{2} = 2(1)(3) + 3(1)(2) = 6 + 6 = 12
$$

Hence,

$$
\begin{align}
\left[ d(\vec{u}, \vec{v}) \right] ^{2} &  = \| \vec{u} \| ^{2} - 2 \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle + \| \vec{v} \| ^{2} \\ \\
 &  = 5 -2 \cdot 12 + 30 \\ \\
 & = 5 - 24 + 30 \\ \\
 & = 11
\end{align}
$$

So, 

$$
d(\vec{u}, \vec{v}) = \sqrt{ 11 }
$$

> [!note]
> While we are used to the **norm**, or *magnitude*, of the vector being calculated as $\| \vec{u} \| = \sqrt{ u_{1}^{2} + u_{2}^{2} + \cdots + u_{n}^{2} }$ with respect to the standard inner product to $R^{n}$. What the length and distance of a vector computes out to be wholly depends on the inner product you are using. 

*Remark*: 

If we were to take the inner product with respect to the standard inner product on $R^{2}$, then we'd find

$$
\| \vec{u}  \| = \sqrt{ 2 }, \| \vec{v} \| = \sqrt{ 13 }, d(\vec{u}, \vec{v}) = \sqrt{ 5 }
$$

### Unit Spheres and Circles

> [!definition]
> If $V$ is an inner product space, then the set of points in $V$ which satisfy $$ \| \vec{u} \| = 1 $$ is called the unit sphere in $V$ (or the unit circle in the case where $R^{2}$).

Alternative forms of the unit sphere equation include:

$$
\| \vec{u} \| ^{2} = 1 \quad \text{or} \quad \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{u}} \right\rangle =1
$$

**Problem:** Sketch the unit circle in $R^{2}$ using the given inner product.

$$
\left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle = \frac{1}{4}u_{1}v_{1} + \frac{1}{16}u_{2}v_{2}
$$

**Approach:** Suppose we had the vector $\vec{x} = (x,y)$ in $R^{2}$. The points on the unit circle in $R^{2}$ with respect to this inner product satisfy $\| \vec{x} \| = 1$ or equivalently $\| \vec{x} \|^{2} = 1$. We compute the squared norm of $\vec{x}$ as follows,

$$
\| \vec{x} \| ^{2} = \frac{1}{4}x^{2} + \frac{1}{16}y^{2}
$$

We recognize the axes of this ellipse as $a^{2} = 4$ or $a = 2$ and $b^{2} = 16$ or $b = 4$. 

Through that we have the vertices, $(-4,0), (4,0), (0,2),(0,-2)$, as our guide in sketching the curve on the $xy$-plane.

## Inner Products Generated By Matrices

> [!definition]
> Let $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ be vectors in $R^{n}$ that are expressed in column form, and let $A$ be an invertible $n \times n$ matrix. It can be shown if $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}$ is the Euclidean inner product on $R^{n}$, then the formula $\left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle = A\vec{u} \cdot A \vec{v}$ is also an inner product on $R^{n}$, called the inner product on $R^{n}$ generated by $A$. 

Since $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ are in column form, then $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}$ can be written as $\vec{v}^{T}\vec{u}$ which follows that the matrix inner product can be written as

$$
A\vec{u} \cdot A\vec{v} = (A\vec{v})^{T}(A\vec{u}) = \vec{v}\ ^{T}A^{T}A\vec{u}
$$

For the standard and weighted inner products on $R^{n}$, they happen to be special cases of matrix inner products. If $A = I$, then the matrix inner product defined on $R^{n}$ is $\left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle = \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}$. The matrix which generates the weighted inner product is

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}
\sqrt{ w_{1} } & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \sqrt{ w_{2} } &  0 & 0 \\
\vdots &  \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \cdots & \sqrt{ w_{n} }
\end{bmatrix}
$$

since $A^{T}A$ is the diagonal matrix with weights $w_{1}, w_{2}, \cdots, w_{n}$ along its diagonal.

> [!note]
> It's important to note that $A$ must be invertible for it to generate an inner product on $R^{n}$; otherwise, it would break the bijection in the Positivity Axiom for inner product spaces. While $\vec{u} = \vec{0}$ would yield $A\vec{u} = \vec{0}$ for any $A \in M_{nn}$, if $A$ is not invertible, there are nontrivial solutions to $A\vec{u} = \vec{0}$. Therefore $\| \vec{u} \| = 0$ guarantees $\vec{u} = \vec{0}$ when $A$ is invertible. 

**Problem:** Let $R^{2}$ have the inner product generated by $A$ where

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

and let $\vec{u} = (1,1)$, $\vec{v} = (3,2)$, and $\vec{w} = (0,-1)$. Compute the stated quantities:

$$
\begin{align}
 & a) & \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {v} \right\rangle \\ \\
 & b) & \left\langle {k\vec{v}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle \\ \\
 & c) & \left\langle {\vec{u} + \vec{v}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle \\ \\
\end{align}
$$

**Approach:** Since we know that the inner product can be written as a matrix product, that is,

$$
\left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle  = A\vec{u} \cdot A\vec{v} = \vec{v}\ ^{T}A^{T}A\vec{u}
$$

Then each inner product calculation shares the matrix $A^{T}A$. Computing that allows us to reuse it in other calculations making our lives easier.

$$
A^{T}A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 3 & 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

*For part a)*

Based on the original formula, the inner product between $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ is calculated as

$$
\begin{align}
\left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle & = \vec{v}\ ^{T}A^{T}A\vec{u} \\ \\
   & = \begin{bmatrix}3 & 2\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}5 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \\ \\
 & = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}8 \\ 5\end{bmatrix} \\ \\
 & = 24 + 10 \\ \\
 & = 34
\end{align}
$$

*For part b)*

It's useful to know that $\left\langle {k\vec{v}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle = k\left\langle {\vec{v}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle$ by Homogeneity Axiom, so we can pull out the scalar and then factor it in at the last step.

$$
\begin{align}
\left\langle {k\vec{v}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle & = k\left\langle {\vec{v}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle \\  \\
 & = k(\vec{w}\ ^{T}A^{T}A\vec{v}) \\ \\
 & = 3 \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}5 & 3 \\ 3 & 2\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} \\ \\
 & = 3 \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 0  & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 21 \\ 13\end{bmatrix} \\ \\
 & = 3 \cdot -13 \\ \\
 & = -39
\end{align}
$$

*For part c)*

Using the additivity of inner products and the inner product on $R^{n}$ to a matrix product,

$$
\begin{align}
\left\langle {\vec{u} + \vec{v}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle & = \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle + \left\langle {\vec{v}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle \\ \\
 & = \vec{w}\ ^{T} A^{T}A\vec{u} + \vec{w}\ ^{T}A^{T}A\vec{v}
\end{align}
$$

so calculations can be reused from steps in part (a) and part (b)

$$
\begin{align}
\left\langle {\vec{u} + \vec{v}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle & = \vec{w}\ ^{T} A^{T}A\vec{u} + \vec{w}\ ^{T}A^{T}A\vec{v} \\ \\
 & = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}8 \\ 5\end{bmatrix} - 13 & \text{Subbing in}\ \vec{w}\ ^{T}, A^{T}A\vec{u},\ \text{and}\ \left\langle {\vec{v}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle\\ \\
  & = -5 - 13 \\ \\
  & = -18 
\end{align}
$$

## Inner Products on Other Spaces

### Square Matrices

The standard inner product on the space $M_{nn}$  is defined as

$$
\left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle = \text{tr}(U^{T}V)
$$

for vectors $\vec{u} = U$ and $\vec{v} = V$ in the vector space of $n \times n$ matrices.

While multiplying matrices and then computing the trace is one way to go to find this inner product, we could consider finding a general formula if the entries of $2 \times 2$ (base case) matrices $U$ and $V$ are as follows:

$$
\begin{align}
\left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle &  = \text{tr}(U^{T}V) \\ \\
 & = \text{tr}\left( \begin{bmatrix} u_{1} & u_{2} \\ u_{3} & u_{4} \end{bmatrix}^{ T} \begin{bmatrix} v_{1} & v_{2} \\ v_{3} & v_{4} \end{bmatrix}\right) \\ \\
  & = \text{tr}\left( \begin{bmatrix} u_{1} & u_{3} \\ u_{2} & u_{4} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} v_{1} & v_{2} \\ v_{3} & v_{4} \end{bmatrix} \right) \\ \\
 & = \text{tr}\left( \begin{bmatrix} u_{1}v_{1} + u_{3}v_{3} & u_{1}v_{2} + u_{3}v_{4} \\ u_{2}v_{1} + u_{4}v_{3} & u_{2}v_{2} + u_{4}v_{4} \end{bmatrix} \right) \\ \\
 & = (u_{1}v_{1} + u_{3}v_{3}) + (u_{2}v_{2} + u_{4}v_{4}) \\ \\
  & = u_{1}v_{1} + u_{2}v_{2} + u_{3}v_{3} + u_{4}v_{4}
\end{align}
$$

So the standard inner product defined on $M_{nn}$ is the dot product between the corresponding entries of $U$ and $V$. 

> It can be shown that this way of computation works for all $n \times n$ matrices. 

The norm of $\vec{u}$ would work as you'd expect in this inner product space: It's the sum of each entry of $U$ squared. 

---

**Problem:** Compute the standard inner product on $M_{22}$ of the given matrices

$$
U = \begin{bmatrix}3 & -2 \\ 4 & 8 \end{bmatrix},\ V = \begin{bmatrix}-1 & 3 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Approach:** If we refer to $\vec{u} = U$ and $\vec{v} = V$ where $\vec{u}, \vec{v} \in M_{22}$ then the inner product is defined as $\text{tr}(U^{T}V)$. As shown in the results before, we do not need to compute $U^{T}V$ and collect the entries along its diagonals. We can simply just do the Euclidean inner product of each corresponding entry between $U$ and $V$. 

$$
\begin{align}
\left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle  & = \text{tr}(U^{T}V) \\ \\
 & = 3(-1) -2(3) + 4(1) + 8(1) \\ \\
 & = -3 -6 + 4 + 8 \\ \\
 & = 3
\end{align}
$$

### Polynomials

If $\vec{p} = a_{0} + a_{1}x + a_{2}x^{2} + \cdots + a_{n}x^{n}$ and $\vec{q} = b_{0} + b_{1}x + b_{2}x^{2} + \cdots + b_{n}x^{n}$ are polynomials in $P_{n}$ then the following formula defines an inner product on $P_{n}$ which is called the *standard inner product* on this space:

$$
\left\langle {\vec{p}}, {\vec{q}} \right\rangle = a_{0}b_{0} + a_{1}b_{1} + \cdots + a_{n}b_{n} 
$$

---

**Problem:** Find the standard inner product on $P_{2}$ of the given polynomials.

$$
\begin{align}
\vec{p} &  = -5 + 2x + x^{2} \\ \\
\vec{q} & = 3 + 2x - 4x^{2} \\
\end{align}
$$

**Approach:** If not aligned, it's best to align the order of the polynomial terms from lowest degree to highest degree. The standard inner product on $P_{2}$ is defined as $\left\langle {\vec{p}}, {\vec{q}} \right\rangle = a_{0}b_{0} + a_{1}b_{1} + a_{2}b_{2}$. And so the inner product for these two polynomials is calculated as

$$
\begin{align}
\left\langle {\vec{p}}, {\vec{q}} \right\rangle &  = -5(3) + 2(2) + 1(-4) \\ \\
 &  = -15 + 4 - 4 \\ \\
  & = -15
\end{align}
$$

---

Another inner product can be defined on $P_{n}$ where if we see $\vec{p} = p(x)$ and $\vec{q} = q(x)$ and $x_{1},x_{2}, ..., x_{n}$ are distinct real numbers, called *sample points*, then the formula given by

$$
\left\langle {\vec{p}}, {\vec{q}} \right\rangle = p(x_{0})q(x_{0}) + p(x_{1})q(x_{1}) + \cdots + p(x_{n})q(x_{n})
$$

defines an inner product on $P_{n}$ called the *evaluation inner product* at $x_{0}, x_{1}, x_{2}, ..., x_{n}$

We can show that the evaluation inner product on $P_{n}$ satisfies the axioms:

1. Symmetry holds because of commutativity of multiplication. 
2. Additivity holds because of associativity of addition and commutativity of multiplication.
3. Homogeneity holds because $k\vec{p} = kp(x)$ implies that $k\vec{p}$ evaluated at $x_{i}$ for $i = 1, 2, ..., n$ is equivalent to $kp(x_{i})$. The final step is to factor out $k$. 
4. Positivity holds since $\|  \vec{p} \| = \sqrt{ [p(x_{0})]^{2} + [p(x_{1})]^{2} + \cdots + [p(x_{n})]^{2} }$ will always be greater than zero for nonzero vectors. The only way for the norm to equate to zero is if $p(x) = 0\ \forall x \in R$,  then $\vec{p} = \vec{0}$; likewise, the converse also is true. 


**Problem:** A sequence of sample points is given. Use the evaluation inner product on $P_{3}$ at those sample points to find $\left\langle {\vec{p}}, {\vec{q}} \right\rangle$ for the polynomials $\vec{p} = x + x^{3}$ and $\vec{q} = 1 + x^{2}$.

$$
x_{0} = -2,x_{1} = -1, x_{2} = 0, x_{3} = 1
$$

**Approach:** We need to see that $\vec{p} = p(x) = x+x^{3}$ in order to evaluate $p$ at the sample points.

$$
\begin{align}
p(x_{0}) &  = p(-2) = -2 + (-2)^{3} = -2 - 8 = -10 \\ \\
p(x_{1}) & = p(-1) = -1 + (-1)^{3} = -1 -1 = -2 \\ \\
p(x_{2}) & = p(0) = 0 + 0^{3} = 0 + 0 = 0 \\ \\
p(x_{3}) & = p(1) = 1 + 1^{3} = 1 + 1 = 2
\end{align}
$$

And likewise for the vector $\vec{q}$, we see that  $\vec{q} = q(x) = 1+x^{2}$. So evaluating $q$ at the $x_{0}, x_{1}, x_{2}, x_{3}$ looks like

$$
\begin{align}
q(x_{0}) &  = q(-2) = 1 + (-2)^{2} = 1 + 4 = 5 \\ \\
q(x_{1}) & = q(-1) = 1 + (-1)^{2} = 1 + 1 = 2 \\ \\
q(x_{2}) & = q(0) = 1 + 0^{2} = 1 + 0 = 1 \\ \\
q(x_{3}) & = q(1) = 1 + 1^{2} = 1 + 1 = 2
\end{align}
$$

So the standard inner product on $P_{3}$ for $\vec{p}$ and $\vec{q}$ is calculated as

$$
\begin{align}
\left\langle {\vec{p}}, {\vec{q}} \right\rangle &  = p(x_{0})q(x_{0}) + p(x_{1})q(x_{1}) + p(x_{2})q(x_{2}) + p(x_{3})q(x_{3}) \\ \\
 & = -10(5) -2(2) + 0(1) + 2(2) \\ \\
 & = -50 - 4 + 4 \\ \\
 & = - 50
\end{align}
$$

### Integral Inner Product

Let $\vec{f} = f(x)$ and $\vec{g} = g(x)$ be two functions in $C[a,b]$ and consider the formula

$$
\left\langle {\vec{f}}, {\vec{g}} \right\rangle = \int_{a}^{b} f(x)g(x) \, dx 
$$

which is called the **integral inner product** on $C[a,b]$.

If $C[a,b]$ has the *integral inner product*, then the norm of a function $\vec{f} = f(x)$ relative to this inner product is

$$
\| \vec{f} \| = \sqrt{ \left\langle {\vec{f}}, {\vec{f}} \right\rangle } = \left[ \int_{a}^{b} f^{2}(x) \, dx  \right]^{1/2}
$$

and the unit sphere in this space consists of all functions $\vec{f} \in C[a,b]$ that satisfy $\int_{a}^{b} f^{2}(x) \, dx = 1$.

> [!note]
> Because all polynomials are continuous everywhere, we can say $P_{n}$ is a subspace of $C[a,b]$. It shows the integral inner product is also defined on $P_{n}$ if defined on $C[a,b]$. So the vector space $P_{n}$ has three distinct inner products that can be defined on it. 

**Problem:** Use the inner product

$$
\left\langle {\vec{f}}, {\vec{g}} \right\rangle = \int_{-1}^{1} f(x)g(x) \, dx
$$
on $C[-1,1]$ to compute for $\vec{f} = \cos 2\pi x$ and $\vec{g} = \sin 2\pi x$ the following the quantities

$$
\begin{align}
 & a) & \left\langle {\vec{f}}, {\vec{g}} \right\rangle \\ \\
 & b) & \|  \vec{f} \| \\ \\
\end{align}
$$

**Approach:**

*For part a)*

It's good to realize that the resulting product $f(x)g(x) = \sin(2\pi x)\cos(2\pi x)$ is in fact an odd function since $\sin(2\pi x)$ is odd and $\cos(2\pi x)$ is even. Moreover, our bounds of integration are symmetric. And we know the net area of an odd function between symmetric bounds is zero. So,

$$
\left\langle {\vec{f}}, {\vec{g}} \right\rangle = \int_{-1}^{1} f(x)g(x) \, dx = \int_{-1}^{1} \sin(2\pi x)\cos(2\pi x) \, dx = 0
$$

*For part b)*

For the norm of $\vec{f}$, we find that $f^{2}(x) = \cos ^{2}(2\pi x)$. Here we can take advantage of Double Angle Identity to integrate this function.

$$
\begin{align}
\| \vec{f} \|  & = \left[ \int_{-1}^{1} f^{2}(x) \, dx  \right]^{1/2} \\ \\
 & = \left[ \int_{-1}^{1} \cos ^{2}(2\pi x) \, dx  \right]^{1/2} \\ \\
 & = \left[ \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{1 + \cos(2 \cdot 2\pi x)}{2} \, dx  \right]^{1/2}  & \text{Double Angle Identity} \\ \\
 & = \left[ \frac{1}{2} \int_{-1}^{1} (1 + \cos 4\pi x) \, dx  \right]^{1/2} \\ \\
 & = \left[ \frac{1}{2} \left( \int_{-1}^{1} 1 \, dx + \int_{-1}^{1} \cos 4\pi x \, dx   \right)  \, dx  \right]^{1/2} \\ \\
 & = \left[ \frac{1}{2} \left( \int_{-1}^{1} 1 \, dx + \frac{1}{4\pi} \sin(4\pi x) \Big|_{-1}^{1}   \right)  \, dx  \right]^{1/2}
\end{align}
$$

At this point we can view the definite integral $\int_{-1}^{1} 1 \, dx$ as the area of a rectangle with height $1$ and width $2$, so the area must be $2$. And we know that $\sin(k\pi x) = 0$ for any positive or negative multiple $k$. Utilizing these ideas,

$$
\begin{align}
\| \vec{f} \|  & = \left[ \frac{1}{2} \left( \int_{-1}^{1} 1 \, dx + \frac{1}{4\pi} \sin(4\pi x) \Big|_{-1}^{1}   \right)  \, dx  \right]^{1/2} \\ \\
 & = \left[ \frac{1}{2} \left( 2 + 0 \right)  \right]^{1/2} \\ \\
 & =\sqrt{ 1 } \\ \\
 & = 1
\end{align}
$$

Hence, $\vec{f}$ is a unit vector on the inner product space formed with $C[-1,1]$.

## Properties of Real Inner Product Spaces

The following theorem is just an extension or restatement of the inner product space axioms.

> [!theorem]
> If $\vec{u}$, $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{w}$ are vectors in a real inner product space $V$, and $k$ is a scalar, then:
> 
> 1. $\left\langle {\vec{0}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle = \left\langle {\vec{v}}, {\vec{0}} \right\rangle = 0$
> 2. $\left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v} + \vec{w}} \right\rangle = \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle + \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle$
> 3.  $\left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v} - \vec{w}} \right\rangle = \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle - \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle$
> 4.  $\left\langle {\vec{u} - \vec{v}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle = \left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle - \left\langle {\vec{v}}, {\vec{w}} \right\rangle$
> 5. $k\left\langle {\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle = \left\langle {k\vec{u}}, {\vec{v}} \right\rangle$
